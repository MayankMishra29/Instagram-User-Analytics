# Chapter 11: When to Talk About the Offer

<p class="lede">The offer should feel like the next sentence, not a new speaker.</p>

Two failure stories, one on each side.

**The identity cliff.** An agency owner has a warm three-week thread with a head of marketing: good questions, real context, genuine rapport. Then message 11 is a pasted service menu with three tiers, a logo wall and a Calendly link. The buyer replies eight days later: *"Will circle back."* The problem wasn't making an offer. It was that a curious, thoughtful person suddenly turned into a brochure. The buyer experienced a **change of identity**, and identity changes feel like a trick.

**The hiding problem.** A buyer describes a problem, asks how you'd approach it and confirms they want to fix it this quarter. You send another educational article because you don't want to sound salesy. Now the buyer is confused. They've invited a commercial conversation, and they need to know what you do, what it costs and whether it fits. Withholding that isn't respect. It's friction.

Both failures have the same fix: **make the offer a continuation of the conversation.**

## When the offer should appear

The offer belongs in the conversation when **readiness signals** are present (Chapter 10): an acknowledged problem, a desired change, a reason to act, and ideally a buyer asking something like *"How would you approach this?"* or *"What would this look like?"*

The offer should **not** appear:

- because you've reached message three
- because they accepted a connection
- because they thanked you for a resource
- because they shared something personal
- for a service you know doesn't fit

When you're unsure, **ask permission**, and make it a real decision point, not a question whose answer you'll ignore:

> "It sounds like this might be worth fixing. Would it help if I explained how I'd approach it, or are you still figuring out whether it's a priority?"

## The recap → reason → request transition

A good transition has three parts:

1. **Recap:** an accurate summary of the problem in the buyer's words.
2. **Reason:** why your work connects to it, and why you're raising it now.
3. **Request:** a clear choice about how to evaluate it, with an easy way to decline.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Consultant:** You want the account team to run kickoff without asking you to reconstruct the sale. That fits the handoff work I do. A sensible scope would be mapping the current process, creating a shared brief, and piloting it with one team. Would you like the scope and price in writing, or would a 20-minute call be easier?
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Connect the service to a confirmed objective |
| **Buyer may experience** | The offer follows logically; nothing appeared from nowhere |

| **Better move** | A concrete scope, in the buyer's preferred format |

The recap must be **true**. Don't exaggerate the problem to make your offer look inevitable. If they wanted one process fix, don't suddenly propose a full transformation without explaining why broader work is needed.

## Explain the work before naming the package

A branded offer name helps people remember. It can't carry the explanation. Use the Clarifier structure:

- **Who it's for** (the buying situation)
- **What changes** (the intended outcome)
- **What you deliver** (concrete outputs)
- **What they put in** (participation and inputs)
- **What's excluded**
- **Price and timeline**

Keep deliverables and outcomes separate. A documented handoff process is a deliverable. Less clarification work is an intended operational outcome. More revenue may depend on many other conditions. Don't slide silently from the first to the third.

## Price is information

When you know your price, **state it**. When scope affects it, give an honest range or name the pricing drivers. Hiding price to protect a meeting is one of the most common trust leaks in B2B selling: the buyer asks what it costs and gets a booking link. A meeting booked through avoidable uncertainty is weak progress.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** What would that cost?

**Consultant:** For the four-week, one-team pilot we discussed: $3,000, one-time, before applicable taxes. That covers the process map, shared brief, ownership rules and a handover session. Company-wide rollout and ongoing management aren't included.
</div>

Include currency, whether it's one-time or recurring, the scope, and material exclusions. (The $3,000 is illustrative. Use a figure you can deliver profitably and justify through the scope.) If the right price needs more discovery, say what drives it and what the smallest useful next step is. "It depends" is not a complete answer.

### "Too expensive"

Clarify once, without implying they don't understand value:

> "Understood. Is it that the budget is lower, or that this scope doesn't seem worth the fee?"

- **Budget is fixed:** offer a smaller scope that still works, a later date, or a clear no. A reduced price must match a real change in scope. Removing the work that makes success possible, just to keep the sale, is not a compromise.
- **Value is unclear:** go back to the evidence and assumptions. Don't recite an inflated ROI calculation.

### "Can you guarantee results?"

Be precise about what you control:

> "I can't guarantee client wins from this work. I can commit to the agreed implementation, testing and reporting. Whether it produces sales also depends on the offer, demand, your team's execution and the buying cycle. Let's agree the leading indicators and review points before we start."

Never invent a guarantee to make an objection disappear. If you do offer a real guarantee, its conditions and remedy belong in the agreement, not improvised in a DM.

## Free help vs. paid diagnosis

A short observation from public information shows how you think. A serious diagnosis needs interviews, internal data and real analysis. Draw the line **before** you do the work:

> "I can share one or two observations from the public side so you can judge my thinking. A full diagnosis needs process interviews and internal data, so that's paid work. I can tell you exactly what it would deliver before you decide."

Don't call a disguised sales pitch an "audit." If you offer a free review, say what it includes and what it doesn't. A boundary isn't pressure when it leaves a real choice.

## Ask about authority without dismissing the person

> "If this looks useful, who else would need to be involved in evaluating it or approving the work?"

The person you're talking to may be the user, the evaluator or the internal champion, even if they can't sign. Help them evaluate. Never push them to bypass their own decision process.

## The Coherence Check

Before you escalate an important opportunity to an offer, check that every surface tells the same story:

| Surface | Question |
|---|---|
| **Profile** | Does it support the identity you've shown in the DM? |
| **Content** | Does it support the beliefs the conversation has created? |
| **Conversation** | Does the offer message sound like the same person who wrote the last three messages? |
| **Offer** | Does it follow naturally from the problem you discussed? |
| **Modality** | Does the format fit the relationship's trust level? |
| **Promise** | Is it believable from where the buyer stands right now? |

If any row fails, trust leaks at the exact moment you need it most.

## Action: the offer bridge

1. Write a two-sentence recap of one buyer's confirmed problem and desired change, using their words.
2. Describe the smallest useful engagement you can actually deliver, in 90 words or fewer, using the Clarifier structure, **with the price**.
3. List one reason it might *not* be the right choice for them.
4. Write a clear invitation to evaluate it, with an easy way to decline.
5. Run the Coherence Check against your profile and your last five posts.

**Stop:** either hiding the offer or introducing it without context. **Start:** connecting it to the buyer's own goal. **Notice:** scope creep and price surprises. **Measure:** fewer proposals that need basic clarification about what's being sold.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Commercial clarity should feel like continuity, not a change of identity.</div>

# Chapter 12: Modality: Text → Voice → Loom → Call

<p class="lede">Escalate the format when it resolves their uncertainty, not yours.</p>

A founder at Gate 3 asks a simple question. A B2B service seller replies with an unrequested seven-minute screen recording. It gets one view: 40 seconds watched.

Compare that with another thread: a buyer asks a specific, nuanced question, and the seller replies with a 45-second voice note answering exactly that question, plus a one-line text summary. The buyer replies within the hour.

Same seller, different results. The difference wasn't the medium. It was whether the medium matched the moment.

## Modality as trust compression

Each step up in richness compresses more trust into less time. You hear tone, see a face, watch a process. That's powerful. It also **costs the buyer more**: attention, scheduling, exposure and the social obligation of engaging with someone who clearly invested effort.

<div class="flow">
<span><b>Text</b><br>Low cost, high control<br><small>Specific answers, recaps, scope</small></span><span><b>Voice</b><br>Adds tone and nuance<br><small>Harder to skim or quote</small></span><span><b>Loom / video</b><br>Shows a process or page<br><small>Needs quiet time to watch</small></span><span><b>Call</b><br>Real-time, connected questions<br><small>Costs time, scheduling, exposure</small></span>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 13. The Modality Ladder. Each rung adds richness and adds cost for the buyer. Moving back down is allowed.</p>

**Modality escalation is a risk ask.** When you suggest a call, you're asking the buyer to take on time risk and social risk. That's fine when the trust state supports it, and friction when it doesn't. Premature escalation is one of the most common reasons warm threads go quiet.

## When each format fits

**Text** is right for a specific answer, a recap, a scope summary or an agreed reminder. It gives the buyer control over timing and leaves a reference they can reread or forward. It gets weak when many connected uncertainties produce long, branching exchanges. Then offer a choice rather than forcing a call.

**Voice** adds tone and makes a nuanced explanation feel less formal. It's also harder to scan, quote or listen to discreetly. Ask before sending anything long, and include a short text summary. Never use a voice note to avoid putting a material promise in writing.

**Loom-style video** is right when the subject is visual or procedural: a page, a workflow, a sequence of decisions. Define a narrow question first. A two-minute recording showing one discrepancy beats a wandering fifteen-minute audit. Ask whether they want it, say how long it is, and add a written summary so they can decide whether to watch.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Designer:** The difference is easier to show on the two booking screens. Would a two-minute screen recording help, or would you prefer a written note?

**Buyer:** Written is easier for me.

**Designer:** Sure. I'll describe the two points and include a marked-up screenshot.
</div>

The buyer's format preference is information, not resistance.

**Calls** are right when a problem has interdependent parts, several people need to align, or real-time back-and-forth will prevent misunderstanding. Make the invitation concrete: **purpose, length, participants, preparation and likely output.**

> "We'd use 20 minutes to map where unsuitable inquiries come in, look at the current booking questions, and decide whether I can help. If it's outside my work, I'll say so."

Once they agree, send a booking link or two real times with dates and time zones. Don't add colleagues or send calendar invites without agreement. Confirm any recording before it starts.

**Calls are a tool, not the prize.** Never push for a call because calls "convert better." If the buyer can make a sound decision from a clear written scope, a call just adds friction. And never ask for a call because you don't know what else to say. That's the Pause Test failing.

## The Modality Readiness Checklist

Before changing format, check:

- ☐ I can say which question this format resolves better than text.
- ☐ The buyer asked for it or agreed to it.
- ☐ The length and preparation are proportionate.
- ☐ There's a written alternative where accessibility or context needs one.
- ☐ Nothing sensitive is on screen or in the recording.
- ☐ For a call: purpose, participants, time zone and output are clear.
- ☐ Any material promise will be confirmed in writing.
- ☐ If they decline the format, I won't turn that into pressure.

If you can't check the first box, the escalation is serving your sales habit, not the buyer.

## Action: the least-burdensome format

Take three current exchanges. For each, identify the open question and compare text, voice, video and a call. Choose the **lowest-effort format that can resolve it accurately**, and write a one-sentence reason. If another format is really needed, ask rather than assume.

**Stop:** treating channel changes as automatic progress. **Start:** matching format to the problem and the buyer's preference. **Notice:** hidden attention costs. **Measure:** fewer abandoned escalations; less ghosting after a call request.

<div class="rule">Field rule: The buyer's trust state, not your conversion rate, decides when it's time for a call.</div>
