# Chapter 1: Why Your DMs Feel Salesy

<p class="lede">You're not trying too hard. You're trying too early.</p>

Jon runs a small business advisory firm. A new connection sends him this:

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** Hey Jon, saw you're a founder. We help founders generate 10–20 qualified leads per month through LinkedIn. We handle content, outreach and booking. Would you be open to a quick call this week?
</div>

No reply.

The rep's instinct is to soften it. Next time they write "Would it be crazy to explore this?" instead of "quick call." Still nothing. So they conclude that Jon is busy, or that LinkedIn is saturated, or that they personally come across as "too salesy," and that the fix is to become a different kind of person.

All three conclusions are wrong. Let's look at what actually happened.

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **What the rep thinks they're doing** | A clear, efficient introduction with a concrete outcome and a small ask |
| **What Jon may experience** | A stranger picked his job title, assigned him a problem he never mentioned, and asked for 30 minutes of his week |
| **Trust diagnosis** | Jon is at **Reality/Relevance** (*who is this, and why me?*). The message tries to pass Relevance, Fit, Value and Safety in 38 words, then asks for a Commitment-level action |
| **Better move** | Find a real context before contacting Jon at all, or open on one relevant observation with no ask attached |
| **Why** | Softer wording can't repair an unsupported premise. The problem isn't tone. It's sequence |
</div>

## The old model: remove the sales words

When people dislike traditional selling, they usually try to make commercial messages sound less commercial. "Sales call" becomes "chat." "Pitch" becomes "share an idea." "Prospect" becomes "new friend." Then they keep pursuing the same pre-selected outcome at the same pace.

That makes things worse. The buyer now has to *discover* the commercial purpose after investing attention. The friendly question turns out to be the entrance to a sequence the sender planned before hearing the answer. Hidden intent reads as manipulation, even when the person means well.

You don't need to pretend business is absent. You need to make the business relevance understandable and leave the buyer free to engage or not.

## Why it breaks: the request arrives before its reason

"Salesy" almost always describes a **mismatch between the seller's agenda and the buyer's current position**:

- The seller acts as if an evaluation is underway. The buyer is still deciding whether the topic matters.
- The seller adds proof. The buyer wants to know what the service actually includes.
- The seller offers a discount. The buyer is worried their team can't implement the work.
- The seller asks for a call. The buyer hasn't yet decided the sender is relevant.

I call this **premature pressure**: a message answering a trust question the buyer hasn't reached yet. It's why the same message can be perfectly appropriate at one point in a conversation and pushy at another. "Here's my price and here's how we'd start" is exactly right when a buyer asks. It's pressure in the second message to a stranger.

This is also why buyers detect salesiness so fast. Professionals receive enough outreach to recognize the pattern of a persuasion attempt in the first line. They aren't reacting to your adjectives. They're reacting to the **gap between the conversation you appear to be having and the sale you're trying to create.**

## The new model: persuasion vs. trust

This book draws a structural distinction:

| | **Persuasion (as usually practiced)** | **Trust** |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Cause movement | Make movement safe |
| **Main tools** | Argument, urgency, objection handling, pressure | Relevance, clarity, accurate belief, reduced risk |
| **Who sets the pace** | The seller's sequence | The buyer's understanding |
| **What a "no" means** | An obstacle to overcome | Information, and sometimes the right answer |

Not all persuasion is bad. Explaining clearly why your approach fits someone's situation is persuasion, and it's necessary. The line is between **helping someone make a decision** and **trying to force decision velocity**. The first increases the quality of the decision. The second only increases its speed, usually by hiding information or inventing urgency, and buyers can feel it.

Trust moves deals. Pressure breaks them.

## The Salesiness Scale

Use this spectrum to inspect *messages*, not to judge yourself as a person:

| Level | Behavior | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| **0 · Human** | Natural, contextual exchange | Is the connection real and relevant? |
| **1 · Helpful** | Useful contribution with no obligation | Is the help secretly a debt? |
| **2 · Commercial** | Explicit business relevance, freedom to decline | Are your scope and intent clear? |
| **3 · Transactional** | Attention turns to terms and a decision | Is this an evaluation the buyer agreed to? |
| **4 · Pushy** | Your agenda overrides their context | Is hesitation being ignored or punished? |
| **5 · Coercive** | Deception, false urgency, boundary violations | Stop and correct it |

**The goal is not to stay at zero.** A serious commercial conversation has to reach a decision. Level 3 is entirely appropriate when both sides are evaluating terms. A plain "Would you like to go ahead with the scope we discussed?" is often *less* pressuring than a supposedly generous stream of unrequested advice.

The target is **commercial clarity without relational pressure.**

## The other failure mode: hiding

Here's the part most "authentic selling" advice gets wrong. If you absorb only the anti-pressure half of this book, you'll overcorrect into **hiding**: never naming a price, never making an offer, "adding value" forever because asking feels salesy.

Hiding is also a trust leak. A buyer who has described a problem, asked how you'd approach it and said they want to fix it this quarter needs to know what you do, what it costs and whether it fits. Sending them another article at that point isn't respectful. It's confusing. Ambiguity is a risk, and risk stops decisions.

Pushing moves faster than the buyer. Hiding moves slower than the buyer. Both ignore where the buyer actually is.

## When more value creates more friction

A founder tells you they're busy. You respond with a long teardown, a video, three examples and a calendar link. You think you've made the decision easier. What you've actually done is create a new task: review everything, figure out what's relevant, and respond to someone who has visibly invested effort and will expect something back.

A gift that implicitly demands repayment isn't experienced as a gift. This is a **trust tax**: unnecessary effort or uncertainty attached to your message. Chapter 3 makes it precise. For now: offer one observation, say what it does and doesn't establish, and let the buyer choose whether they want more.

## The Decay Loop, and how to interrupt it

Salesiness usually escalates through a loop, and the loop usually starts inside the seller:

<div class="flow loop">
<span>Silence</span><span>Seller anxiety</span><span>More pressure</span><span>Buyer withdrawal</span><span>More silence</span>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 2. The Decay Loop. Pressure → Resistance → More Pressure → More Resistance, and it begins with the seller's discomfort, not the buyer's behavior.</p>

Silence creates anxiety. The seller raises the stakes: adds urgency, repeats the request, sends more "value." The buyer now has more reason to disengage. The seller reads that as a need for stronger persuasion, and around it goes.

You don't interrupt the loop with better adjectives. You interrupt it by **changing the decision**: go back to the last thing the buyer actually signaled, check whether any next message has a legitimate job, and if it doesn't, pause. If you created a misunderstanding, correct it once. If the buyer said no, stop.

After a premature call request, a repair can be as simple as:

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** I jumped to a call before checking whether this is something you even want to explore. I'll leave it here.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Repair the overreach |
| **Buyer may experience** | Their freedom to disengage is handed back |
| **Trust diagnosis** | The rep created avoidable pressure and is now removing it |
| **Better move** | Acknowledge without attaching a new request |
| **Why** | An apology followed by a new pitch just repeats the original problem |
</div>

## Commercial clarity, done right

Now change the context. Jon has written publicly that referrals have slowed and he's considering a more consistent acquisition route.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** Jon, your post about referrals slowing made me curious what you're trying to replace. I work on acquisition systems for advisory firms. Are you after more opportunities right now, or mainly less dependence on one source?

**Jon:** Less dependence. We've got enough work for the next two months.

**Rep:** That changes the priority. Sounds like you're planning ahead rather than filling a gap.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Understand the business reason behind the post |
| **Jon may experience** | The sender actually read his point, and allowed an answer that reduces the immediate sale |
| **Trust diagnosis** | Relevance is plausible; timing is unknown. Jon is at **Relevance → Fit** |
| **Better move** | Acknowledge the distinction before suggesting anything |
| **Why** | The buyer's answer should change the conversation, not just trigger the next prepared line |
</div>

The rep might next ask when Jon wants to evaluate alternatives, or share one relevant consideration and leave it there. What they must not do is ignore the answer and pitch an emergency pipeline fix.

Notice that the rep said what they do in the first message. That's Level 2 on the scale: commercial, clear, and free to decline. Nothing is hidden and nothing is forced.

## Diagnostic: the job count

Take your last sent message and underline each job it tries to do:

- Establish who you are?
- Prove expertise?
- Ask about a problem?
- Explain the offer?
- Request time?
- Create urgency?

More than one job isn't automatically wrong, but more than two should make you check whether the buyer asked for that much. Then answer three questions:

1. Which uncertainty did the buyer actually express?
2. Does my message address that uncertainty, or my own discomfort with an undecided sale?
3. What would a proportionate response look like if this person never bought from me?

## Action: the Salesiness Audit

Pull your last 10 sent DMs. For each one:

1. Score it 0–5 on the Salesiness Scale.
2. Tag it: **too early**, **too much**, **wrong signal**, **hiding**, or **fine**.
3. For anything tagged "too early," write the one trust question the buyer was probably still answering.

Most people find their problem isn't a personality. It's a pattern: they're consistently one or two gates ahead of the buyer.

**Stop:** disguising a pitch with friendly wording. **Start:** checking whether each request has earned its place. **Notice:** when the buyer's answer contradicts your planned direction. **Measure:** fewer messages containing unsupported diagnoses or uninvited escalations. Count the errors. Don't assume a higher reply rate proves more trust. I've seen a 30% reply rate sitting on top of a conversion problem.

<div class="rule">Field rule: If you can't explain why the buyer needs your next message, don't send it yet.</div>
