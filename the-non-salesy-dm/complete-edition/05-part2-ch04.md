# Chapter 4: Stop Reading Messages. Read States.

<p class="lede">Read before you move.</p>

A prospect replies: *"Sounds good."*

Your colleague calls it buying intent. You call it politeness. Neither reading is established by those two words.

Look at what came before. If you'd asked whether Tuesday at 10 works, "sounds good" almost certainly accepts a time. If you'd described a service they never asked about, it probably just acknowledges your message. **The meaning lives in the exchange, not in the phrase.**

## The old model: classify the keyword

Keyword thinking maps "interesting" to interest, "expensive" to a price objection and silence to rejection. It's convenient for automation and unreliable for judgment. It ignores what the buyer was responding to, how much commitment you asked for, and whether they answered the substantive question at all.

## The new model: buyer states

A **buyer state** is your best reading of how someone is engaging with *this decision*, *right now*. It's not a personality type, a psychological assessment or special access to their feelings. Someone can be curious about your idea, skeptical of your evidence and unable to buy, all at once.

Always keep two separate notes: **what you observed** and **what it might mean**. "Asked for the implementation steps" is an observation. "Ready to buy" is an interpretation that needs more evidence.

### The Buyer State Map

| State | Evidence that may support it | Low-pressure next move |
|---|---|---|
| **Curious** | Asks about the idea without connecting it to their business | Explain briefly; let interest stay noncommercial |
| **Skeptical** | Questions a claim, comparison or result | Address that specific evidence gap, and its limits |
| **Guarded** | Declines detail or asks why you need to know | Explain your purpose; make disclosure optional |
| **Exploratory** | Shares context and examines possible approaches | Reflect the situation; clarify one important unknown |
| **Overwhelmed** | Mentions capacity or complexity | Simplify the decision or pause; don't add assets |
| **Interested** | Connects your offer to a relevant goal | Ask whether evaluating it is a current priority |
| **Hesitant** | Names an unresolved concern about proceeding | Clarify the concern without arguing it away |
| **Ready** | Asks about concrete scope, approvals, timing or terms | Answer directly; establish the real decision process |
| **Disengaged** | Declines explicitly, or repeatedly leaves no next step | Respect it; close the active record |

These are readings, not rules. A procurement question might be routine information gathering. Detailed context might reflect generosity rather than intent. A slow reply might just mean a busy week.

## Work in probabilities, without inventing numbers

"Working with probabilities" doesn't mean assigning percentages. It means holding several explanations at once and updating as evidence arrives. Use plain confidence labels:

- **Directly stated:** they said it.
- **Strongly suggested:** their behavior points clearly this way.
- **Plausible:** it fits, but so do other readings.
- **Unknown:** you don't have enough.

"Timing is a constraint: *directly stated*. Interest in evaluating later: *plausible*, not confirmed." That one line stops a vague "maybe later" from turning into a scheduled campaign.

## The Two-Hypothesis Reply

This is the most useful habit in this chapter. When a signal is ambiguous:

1. Write down the **two most likely states**.
2. Choose a move that is **appropriate under both**.

If someone says "Interesting, send me more," they might be actively evaluating or politely wrapping up. A short, relevant answer to what they asked works for both. A surprise call request works for only one and adds friction to the other.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** The workshop helps a team separate two offers that buyers confuse.

**Buyer:** Interesting. We have a few overlaps.

**Rep:** When you say overlaps, do buyers struggle to choose, or is it mainly confusing inside the team?

**Buyer:** Inside the team. Customers seem fine.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **What the rep thinks they're doing** | Clarifying a signal before pitching |
| **Buyer may experience** | A relevant distinction instead of an immediate sales leap |
| **Trust diagnosis** | Hypothesis A: curious. Hypothesis B: has a customer-facing problem. The question works under both, and the answer rules out B |
| **Better move** | Update the problem definition. A customer-choice workshop may be the wrong fix for an internal ownership issue |
| **Why** | A correction is information, not resistance to overcome |
</div>

The rep must *not* continue with "Exactly, that's why you need our conversion workshop." The buyer just told them that isn't the problem.

## Read the change, not just the latest message

A single message is a snapshot. Movement tells you more:

- The buyer shifts from general questions to questions about **their team**: exploration may be deepening.
- The buyer stops answering the substantive question **right after you mention a meeting**: that may be friction, or just timing.
- The buyer **corrects your assumption**: your model of their situation needs updating.
- The buyer **volunteers context you didn't ask for**: trust is growing.

You can notice a change without claiming to know its cause. Compare the last two exchanges and ask what's different.

Two things to stop assuming: **short writing isn't low trust** (some people write briefly everywhere), and **warmth isn't readiness** (a friendly buyer may be perfectly happy with their current provider).

## The boundary on emotional reading

Noticing that a buyer seems guarded or cautious is useful. Announcing a private emotional diagnosis is not: "You're afraid to invest," "You've been burned and now you're scared." Those statements turn a reasonable concern into a character judgment.

Stay close to what they said. "You mentioned the last project took more time than expected" is accurate and safe. Ask what would make the process clearer; don't tell the buyer how to feel. And if someone shares something personal, never turn it into sales leverage. Keep the conversation on the business decision and give them room.

## Signals worth recognizing

| Signal | What it supports | What it doesn't establish |
|---|---|---|
| Profile view or like | Possible exposure or topic interest | A problem, or permission for repeated contact |
| "Thanks!" | Courtesy | Engagement with the topic |
| "Interesting" | Acknowledgment or curiosity | Evaluation |
| A detailed description of their situation | Exploration, or a wish to be understood | A problem your work fits |
| Questions about proof | Skepticism or careful evaluation | Rejection |
| Questions about workload or process | Hesitation, or practical planning | Low interest |
| Questions about price, terms, start date | Evaluation or readiness | Authorized approval |
| A clear "no" | A boundary | A hidden buying signal. Stop. |

## Action: the two-interpretation drill

Take your last five short replies. For each one:

1. Write two plausible interpretations, and the context supporting each.
2. Write one response that would be reasonable under both.
3. If no response helps under both, choose to wait.

Then take one confident diagnosis you made last week and ask: *what would disprove it?* If the answer is "nothing," you're protecting an interpretation, not testing it.

**Stop:** assigning intent from isolated phrases. **Start:** writing observation and interpretation separately. **Notice:** changes in the substance of the exchange. **Measure:** how often you updated your view after a buyer correction instead of pushing on.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Infer lightly. Respond to evidence.</div>

# Chapter 5: The Six Trust Gates

<p class="lede">Solve the question the buyer is actually asking before you ask for the decision you want.</p>

A founder asks: *"Have you worked with a company like ours?"* The seller replies with a calendar link.

The buyer asked for evidence of fit. The seller offered a different channel. Changing the channel didn't answer the question. It told the buyer that their question didn't matter much.

The Six Trust Gates exist to catch exactly this mismatch.

## The gates

Buyers move through a sequence of trust questions. Each question has to be answered well enough before the next commitment feels safe.

<div class="gates">
<div class="gate"><b>G1 · Reality</b><span>"Is this person or thing real?"</span></div>
<div class="gate"><b>G2 · Relevance</b><span>"Is this relevant to me?"</span></div>
<div class="gate"><b>G3 · Fit</b><span>"Do they actually understand my situation?"</span></div>
<div class="gate"><b>G4 · Safety</b><span>"Is it safe to explore this?"</span></div>
<div class="gate"><b>G5 · Value</b><span>"Is this worth the investment?"</span></div>
<div class="gate"><b>G6 · Identity</b><span>"Is this the right decision for me/us?"</span></div>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 8. The Six Trust Gates. Sequential in logic, not always in time. A buyer can arrive further along, or step back.</p>

Three things to know before we go gate by gate:

1. **Gates can be passed before the DM.** Your content and profile often settle Reality and Relevance before you ever send a message. That's the 3Cs changing what your first message needs to do.
2. **Gates can reopen.** A new requirement can send a buyer back to Fit. An unexpected contract term can send them back to Safety at the very end. That's **gate regression**, and it's normal.
3. **Use the earliest unresolved gate that actually blocks the next step.** Don't manufacture uncertainty so someone can walk through your model. If a buyer already knows you and asks for the price, give the price.

Never say "you're at Gate 4" to a buyer. The model is for your thinking. The conversation stays in plain language.

---

## G1 · Reality: "Is this person or thing real?"

The buyer is checking legitimacy and coherence. Usually not "are you a fraud?" but "do your identity and claims make sense?"

- **Observable signals:** Questions about who you are, your company or how you found them. Confusion about whether you're recruiting, selling or researching. A profile visit right after your message.
- **Common rep mistake:** Answering with inflated authority claims instead of a plain explanation.
- **What increases trust:** An accurate identity, a coherent profile, a truthful reason for contacting them, proof with a clear origin.
- **What decreases trust:** A vague affiliation, a fabricated referral, an unexplained link, a message that contradicts your profile.
- **Moves:** Clarifier, relevant Self-Disclosure.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** Do we know each other?

**Consultant:** We haven't met. I saw your public post about proposal approvals. I work on that process for service teams, which is why I asked.

**Buyer:** Got it. Thought you might be someone from the event.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Remove identity confusion |
| **Buyer may experience** | The relationship is described accurately |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G1 is the live question; no commercial interest is evident yet |
| **Better move** | Clarify without implying familiarity |
| **Why** | Legitimacy starts with accuracy, not with making the contact sound warmer than it is |
</div>

The less credible answer, "We're connected through a few people," when no introduction exists, fails G1 the moment they check. Mutual connections aren't endorsements. A logo isn't a partnership.

**Diagnostic:** Open your profile next to your last opening message. Could a stranger explain who you are, what you do and why you contacted them without resolving a contradiction? Fix the contradiction before you rewrite the call to action.

<div class="gate-rule">Gate rule: Make your identity easy to verify and hard to misunderstand.</div>

---

## G2 · Relevance: "Is this relevant to me?"

A person can accept that you're legitimate and still have no reason to discuss your topic. Relevance connects your observation to their business. It doesn't require proving they have a problem before you've even talked.

- **Observable signals:** "Why are you asking?" "We don't do that." "Not my area." Or a question about how the idea applies to them.
- **Common rep mistake:** Treating a title, location or company size as evidence of need.
- **What increases trust:** A specific public statement, a requested resource, a real introduction, a clearly described situation.
- **What decreases trust:** Ignoring their business model; sending the same diagnosis to every founder.
- **Moves:** Clarifier (connect the context), a small Insight Gift (let them judge applicability).

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Agency owner:** We don't need more leads.

**Rep:** Understood. Your post was about unsuitable inquiries eating account-team time, and I was asking about qualification, not volume. If that's already solved, there may be nothing here.

**Agency owner:** Qualification's still an issue, actually. We get too many requests for one-off work.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Correct a topic mismatch |
| **Buyer may experience** | Their stated boundary was heard |
| **Trust diagnosis** | The opener's wording obscured relevance; the correction restores it |
| **Better move** | Name the specific issue and leave room for it to already be solved |
| **Why** | A relevant distinction beats arguing that every company needs leads |
</div>

If the owner had replied "No, we changed the form and it's fine," accept it. **Relevance has to be allowed to fail.** Otherwise your question was only a rhetorical entrance to a pitch.

**Diagnostic:** Write "This may be relevant because…" using a verified fact. Then write the answer that would make your offer irrelevant. If you can't imagine that answer, your targeting is too broad.

<div class="gate-rule">Gate rule: The buyer should never have to invent the connection between your message and their situation.</div>

---

## G3 · Fit / Understanding: "Do they actually understand my situation?"

Relevance gets you into the right subject. Fit determines whether your understanding is specific enough to support useful advice. This is where generic discovery fails: the seller hears one familiar symptom and assumes the rest of the story.

- **Observable signals:** Corrections, qualifications, comparisons with a previous attempt, "Our situation's a bit different." These aren't objections. They're data about the conditions your work must succeed in.
- **Common rep mistake:** Forcing every answer into your standard diagnosis.
- **What increases trust:** An accurate recap, a meaningful distinction, a question tied to their last answer, willingness to revise your recommendation.
- **What decreases trust:** "I totally understand" followed by a generic solution.
- **Moves:** Mirror (reflect the meaning, not every word), Clarifier (name the distinction that would change your approach).

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Coach:** I don't struggle to book consultations. People just expect free advice.

**Rep:** So the issue isn't filling the calendar. It's that people show up expecting a different kind of conversation.

**Coach:** Exactly. The invite says "clarity session" and they think that means coaching.

**Rep:** That wording might be creating the expectation. Before touching acquisition, I'd look at what the invitation promises.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Understand the mismatch |
| **Buyer may experience** | The rep resisted the generic "you need more leads" answer |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G3 is being passed through an accurate distinction |
| **Better move** | Examine the promise before recommending more traffic |
| **Why** | The same surface symptom (bad sales calls) can have several causes |
</div>

Notice "might." A useful observation narrows the investigation without claiming a complete diagnosis.

**Diagnostic:** Summarize the buyer's situation in two sentences (current reality, desired change). Ask if it's accurate. Record the correction. Don't write a proposal until its main premise survives that correction.

<div class="gate-rule">Gate rule: Understanding is shown by an accurate distinction, not by saying "I understand."</div>

---

## G4 · Safety: "Is it safe to explore this?"

Safety means understandable commitments and manageable exposure, not the absence of all risk. Buyers want to know whether a call will turn into a hard pitch, whether an "audit" needs sensitive access, or whether accepting a resource creates an obligation.

- **Observable signals:** Questions about what happens next, what they have to provide, whether they can say no, or how much time it takes.
- **Common rep mistake:** Treating these as reluctance to overcome instead of requests for process clarity.
- **What increases trust:** Stating the purpose, duration, inputs, boundaries and possible outcomes of the next step.
- **What decreases trust:** Surprise participants, unnecessary information requests, a "free review" that turns out to be a sales presentation.
- **Moves:** Clarifier; Self-Disclosure if you contributed to the uncertainty.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Founder:** What would you need from us for this review?

**Consultant:** For the first conversation, just a description of the workflow and a public example if you have one. No account access. We'd use 20 minutes to check whether the problem fits my work, then decide whether any further investigation is worth it.

**Founder:** Good. I don't want to share our CRM yet.

**Consultant:** You don't need to. If a later engagement needed access, we'd agree the scope and permissions separately.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Make the next step understandable |
| **Buyer may experience** | A concern about access is respected before they had to fight for it |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G4: information exposure and size of commitment |
| **Better move** | State what is and isn't needed |
| **Why** | An explicit boundary is more reassuring than a broad promise that it'll be easy |
</div>

Don't overdo reassurance. "No pressure, no pressure, absolutely no pressure" makes the pressure more noticeable. **Show the boundary in the design of the next step**, not in the adjectives.

Safety can also be damaged by a "gift." An unsolicited 10-minute video that implies a reply adds work and social obligation. Ask before you create it.

**Diagnostic:** Describe your next step as the buyer will experience it: time, preparation, people, information, output and exit. Circle anything they'd only discover after agreeing. Disclose it or remove it.

<div class="gate-rule">Gate rule: Make the next commitment smaller in uncertainty, not just smaller in wording.</div>

---

## G5 · Value: "Is this worth the investment?"

By now the buyer understands your service and feels comfortable discussing it. The question is whether the expected value justifies the **total** investment (money, participation, disruption and opportunity cost) compared with the alternatives, including doing nothing.

- **Observable signals:** Questions about price, scope, expected results, alternatives, effort, or what happens if it doesn't work.
- **Common rep mistake:** Treating all comparison as price resistance and responding with a discount or a bigger promise.
- **What increases trust:** Relevant evidence, clear scope, realistic outcomes, explicit dependencies, an honest comparison with smaller alternatives.
- **What decreases trust:** Inflated ROI math, cherry-picked case studies, unclear fees, refusing to answer pricing questions.
- **Moves:** Story (true or clearly labeled) to make the mechanism concrete; Clarifier to separate what you control from what depends on the buyer.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** Why would we pay for a diagnostic instead of just fixing the booking form ourselves?

**Consultant:** If the form is the only issue and you know what to change, you probably don't need me. The paid work helps when the mismatch could start in several places (positioning, the offer page, the form or the handoff) and you need evidence before deciding what to fix.

**Buyer:** We don't know where it starts. That's the part we need help with.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Explain where the value boundary is |
| **Buyer may experience** | A simpler alternative acknowledged, not dismissed |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G5 is about whether the work is *necessary*, not just its price |
| **Better move** | Say when the engagement is and isn't worth it |
| **Why** | Buyers decide better when they can compare real options, and they trust sellers who name them |
</div>

Don't calculate revenue leakage from a public symptom. If the buyer gives you volumes and time costs, show your assumptions and keep "capacity freed up" separate from "revenue gained." A defensible estimate beats a dramatic one.

**Diagnostic:** List the alternatives the buyer has, including doing nothing for now. Explain the conditions under which your offer is the better choice. If you can't, clarify the offer before strengthening the pitch.

<div class="gate-rule">Gate rule: Value is a comparison in the buyer's context, not an adjective in your proposal.</div>

---

## G6 · Identity / Commitment: "Is this the right decision for me?"

A buyer can accept all the logic and still need to *own* the commitment. They may need alignment with a partner, confidence their team will participate, or a clear sense of what choosing this says about their priorities.

**"Identity" must never become a lever.** Don't imply that serious founders buy, that courageous people invest, or that hesitation means lack of ambition. That's Level 5 on the Salesiness Scale. The real question is whether the decision fits the buyer's responsibilities and the way they want to work.

- **Observable signals:** Talk of internal alignment, approval, timing, ownership, contract terms, final reservations.
- **Common rep mistake:** Treating a positive evaluation as permission to push for an immediate signature.
- **What increases trust:** A precise recap, explicit responsibilities, a legitimate decision process, space to resolve the last issue.
- **What decreases trust:** Surprise conditions, invented deadlines, attempts to go around a colleague whose approval matters.
- **Moves:** Mirror (acknowledge the weight of the decision), Clarifier (make the commitment concrete).

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** I think it makes sense. I need our operations lead to be comfortable owning it.

**Consultant:** That matters, because the work depends on a named owner. Would a short scope summary for the two of you help, or would you rather discuss it internally first?

**Buyer:** Send the summary. We'll review Thursday.

**Consultant:** I'll include responsibilities, review points and what's excluded. Happy to check in after Thursday if that's useful.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Support an informed internal decision |
| **Buyer may experience** | Their responsibility to involve their team is respected |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G6: ownership and commitment. Earlier agreement on value doesn't settle it |
| **Better move** | Provide a decision aid and agree the next step |
| **Why** | A sale that bypasses the implementation owner often fails after the signature |
</div>

**Diagnostic:** Define what "yes" means in this opportunity. Agreement to a call? Approval of scope? Signed terms? Completed payment? Record which decisions remain, instead of treating all positive language as the same commitment.

<div class="gate-rule">Gate rule: Commitment is real when the buyer owns the decision and the required actions are explicit.</div>

## Using the gates without turning them into a script

The gates are a map of questions, not a way to control people. A past client may start at G5. A referral may arrive with Reality and Relevance settled and Fit wide open. When several concerns show up at once, acknowledge all of them, pick the one blocking the next decision, and answer the others as needed.

<div class="table-caption">Six Gate Quick Reference</div>

| Gate | Buyer's question | Typical signal | Common mistake | Best first move |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G1 Reality | Is this real? | "Do we know each other?" | Authority inflation | Clarifier |
| G2 Relevance | Is this relevant to me? | "Why are you asking?" | Title = need | Clarifier / Insight Gift |
| G3 Fit | Do they understand me? | "Our situation's different" | Standard diagnosis | Mirror |
| G4 Safety | Is it safe to explore? | "What would you need from us?" | Reassurance adjectives | Clarifier |
| G5 Value | Is it worth it? | "Why not just do it ourselves?" | Discount or bigger promise | Story / Clarifier |
| G6 Identity | Is this right for us? | "I need my partner on board" | Pushing for signature | Mirror + Clarifier |

## Action: the Six Gate Diagnostic

Pick three threads: one new, one stalled, one progressing. For each, write:

1. The buyer's latest substantive question or signal
2. The likely gate, and your evidence
3. An alternative gate it could be
4. The smallest useful response
5. One reason you might stop

If you have no evidence for a gate, write "unknown." That's a finding, and it tells you what to ask.

**Stop:** jumping to a later commitment because the conversation feels warm. **Start:** matching your response to the open question. **Notice:** when new information reopens an earlier gate. **Measure:** fewer unanswered buyer questions and clearer evidence behind every escalation.

<div class="rule">Field rule: The buyer's pace is part of the sale.</div>
