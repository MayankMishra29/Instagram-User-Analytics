# Chapter 2: The Inbox Isn't a Funnel

<p class="lede">Don't manage leads. Manage trust states.</p>

Rhea, a freelance designer, opens her tracker on Monday morning. It says: **Follow up: Daniel.**

The actual thread tells a different story. Three weeks ago Daniel said his company would reconsider the website after a product launch, asked her to come back in November, and explained that the team had no capacity for another project.

The tracker remembers a *lead*. The thread contains a *person*, a *promise*, a *constraint* and an *agreed pace*.

If Rhea sends "Just checking whether you had a chance to look at my proposal," she creates a small but real contradiction: she seems not to remember what Daniel told her. The problem isn't the phrase "checking in." The problem is that her system threw away the information she needed to make a good decision.

## What a funnel shows you, and what it hides

Funnels are useful for aggregate questions: how many relevant conversations started, how many qualified, how many proposals were accepted. Keep those numbers. Chapter 14 will make them more honest.

But a funnel is a terrible model for an *individual conversation*. It treats every person as a token that has to move forward through fixed stages on your schedule. Real relationships pause, reverse, go dormant, come back, change direction, and sometimes turn out valuable without ever producing a sale. One person becomes a client. Another becomes a referral source. Another becomes a peer whose comments bring you three clients over two years. A funnel can't see any of that. It only sees "not converted."

## The new model: the Trust Graph

**The inbox is a trust graph.**

Each person is a node. Each node carries state:

- **Memory:** what they remember about you, and how it felt
- **Signal history:** what they've actually said and done
- **Trust level:** described in evidence, not a score
- **Pacing:** the tempo they've set, and any agreed timing
- **Readiness:** whether a decision is possible now
- **Perceived risk:** what they're protecting
- **Open questions:** what's unresolved between you

And the nodes are connected. The person who commented on your post last week knows the person you're about to message. A client you served well is two introductions from your next three buyers. A clumsy message to one person doesn't stay in one thread. It becomes part of how you're remembered in a network.

That's why the core instruction of this chapter is: **don't manage leads, manage trust states.** A lead has one attribute, "not yet converted." A trust state has the attributes you actually need to decide what to do next.

## Every message is one of six things

When you send a message, it does at least one of these jobs in the graph:

| Function | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Trust deposit** | Gives them a reason to see you as useful, accurate, reliable or respectful | Keeping a small promise; admitting your offer doesn't fit |
| **Trust withdrawal** | Undermines those expectations | Forgetting a constraint; overstating proof; pushing past a "no" |
| **Pacing test** | Checks their tempo without forcing it | "Is this something you're exploring now, or later?" |
| **Signal clarification** | Resolves an ambiguous signal | "When you say overlap, is that for buyers or inside the team?" |
| **Memory anchor** | Makes a prior interaction easy to recall | "You mentioned the handoff issue after the launch…" |
| **Rehydration event** | Re-enters a dormant thread in context | Returning at the milestone they named |

Before you send anything, know which of these you intend. If the honest answer is "none of these, I just want a reply," that's a withdrawal.

**One clarification on deposits.** Trust isn't a bank balance you can cash in. Five useful comments don't entitle you to one pitch. The metaphor is useful because it reminds you that every interaction changes the relationship. It's dangerous if you start keeping score. Record trust as evidence: "has seen one work sample and discussed the problem" tells you what to do next. "Trust: 8/10" doesn't.

## What the relationship record should keep

Store the minimum context you need to act well:

- **The last substantive buyer signal**, in their words where possible
- **The current state**, with your uncertainty noted
- **The open question** or likely trust gap
- **Evidence already exchanged**, and its limits
- **The agreed next action**, with its date or trigger
- **Contact boundaries**, including any refusal

Separate fact from interpretation. "Asked to revisit after the launch" is a fact. "Probably anxious about growth" is an inference, usually an unnecessary one. "Not serious" is a judgment that will make your next message worse. Keep private-life details out entirely. A relationship record is not a dossier.

## Silence is data, not rejection

Most funnels have one response to silence: follow up on day 3, day 7, day 14. The Trust Graph has a different stance. **Silence is information, and it's ambiguous information.** It can mean busy, uncertain, uninterested, overwhelmed, evaluating, protecting themselves, or simply forgetting.

You can't know which. So the skill isn't guessing correctly. It's choosing a move that stays respectful under *every* plausible reading, and recognizing when no move is the right one. Chapter 10 goes deep on this. For now, two rules:

1. **No immediate action doesn't mean no trust.** People read, remember and return on their own schedule.
2. **Silence is not permission to escalate indefinitely.**

## The Pause Test

This is the discipline that runs through the entire book. Before any message, ask:

> **Am I sending this because it helps the buyer, or because I'm uncomfortable with the silence?**

If it's the second, pause. Then check the practical versions:

- Am I trying to rush a gate?
- Am I trying to relieve my own uncertainty?
- Am I adding "value" because I'm afraid they won't respond?
- Am I asking for a call because I don't know what else to say?
- Am I following up because the buyer signaled readiness, or because my sequence says it's time?
- Am I answering an objection they haven't raised?
- Am I creating urgency that doesn't exist?

The Pause Test prevents unnecessary pressure. It **does not** excuse withholding a direct answer. If the buyer asked you something, answer it.

A buyer who has read six of your posts arrives in the DM with Reality and Relevance partly settled. A buyer who arrives cold needs the profile to do that work. The best example I've seen is almost embarrassingly simple: an expert added a booking link to a LinkedIn post and received a booking. No sequence, no DM, no funnel. The content created the memory, the profile confirmed it, and the buyer moved on their own. That's the Trust Graph working without any pushing at all.

## Action: the Trust Graph Audit

Review 20 existing conversations, or all of them if you have fewer. Experienced operators can do 50 in batches. **Do not contact anyone as part of the audit.** For each thread, record:

| Field | What to write |
|---|---|
| Last buyer signal | Their actual words or action, with the date |
| Current state | Active evaluation / exploring / invited revisit / dormant / peer / done / do-not-contact |
| Trust evidence | What's been established (e.g., "saw sample, discussed problem") |
| Open question | What's unresolved |
| Promises | Anything *you* said you'd do |
| Next appropriate move | Including "none" and "close the record" |

Use "unknown" rather than inventing an emotional explanation. The Trust Graph Tracker in the Implementation System has this built in, with dropdowns.

You'll almost certainly find three things: promises you forgot, "follow-ups" that would have contradicted what the buyer told you, and dormant threads that are warmer than you assumed.

**Stop:** letting due dates erase conversation context. **Start:** reading the last meaningful exchange before every message. **Notice:** whether your next move honors what was already said. **Measure:** fewer missed promises, fewer wrong follow-ups, fewer active records with no defensible next action.
