# Chapter 6: One Touch. One Trust Job.

<p class="lede">Don't ask "what message should I send?" Ask "what signal is missing?"</p>

A buyer asks what a workshop includes. The seller replies with the agenda, a bio, three case studies, a limited-time offer, a question about budget and a booking link.

The buyer now has six things to interpret instead of one answer to use.

The seller believes completeness creates confidence. Sometimes it does. But completeness *relative to the buyer's question* is very different from completeness relative to everything *you* want to say.

## The old model: maximize every touch

If every message feels like a scarce chance to convert, you'll cram every persuasive asset into it: establish authority, prove expertise, explain the offer, pre-empt objections, create urgency, ask for a call. The result is a message with competing jobs. The recipient has to work out what matters, what's being asked, and whether replying commits them to something bigger.

That's cognitive and emotional overload. Each extra job adds a small uncertainty ("do I need to respond to the case studies? Is the discount real? Is the budget question a qualification test?"), and small uncertainties add up to a trust tax large enough that the easiest response is no response.

## The One-Gate Rule

**One touch, one trust job.** Identify the primary open question and design the message to address it.

This isn't a word-count rule. A two-line message can hide three demands. A long answer is fine when the buyer asked for detail. The test is whether every part of the message serves the *same* decision.

Before drafting, finish this sentence: *"After reading this, the buyer should understand…"* Write an understanding, not a behavior you want to force. "Understand the inputs the review requires" is a clear job. "Book a call, believe my authority, accept the price and get excited" is a wish list.

The focused answer doesn't guarantee a yes. It lets the buyer make a *better* decision, including deciding the review isn't needed. That's the point.

## Exceptions that keep the principle intact

- **The buyer asks three connected questions.** Answer all three. "What's the fee, what's included and when can you start?" is one evaluation. A short, structured answer is right. Never ration information to protect a sequence.
- **Several concerns surface at once.** Name them and prioritize: "There are two things here: whether the method fits, and whether the team has capacity. Capacity sounds like the immediate constraint. Want to settle that first?"
- **You need to correct something.** Correct it now. No model justifies leaving a misleading impression in place until the next scheduled touch.

## Signal Matching

The One-Gate Rule tells you to do one job. **Signal Matching** tells you which job.

Stop asking *"What should I send?"* Ask *"What signal is missing?"* Then work through:

- What does the buyer **currently believe**?
- What don't they **believe yet**?
- What **emotion** is present, and what **fear**?
- What are they **protecting**?
- What would **create safety**?
- What did the **last message establish**?
- What must the next message **not disrupt**?

## The TRUST Loop

Everything from Chapters 2–6 now fits into one five-step habit, the practical core of this book:

<div class="trust-loop">
<div><b>T</b>rack the state<span>What's happening, emotionally and behaviorally? What did they actually say?</span></div>
<div><b>R</b>ecognize the gate<span>Which trust question are they trying to answer?</span></div>
<div><b>U</b>nderstand the risk<span>What could make moving feel unsafe? Which TU term is weak?</span></div>
<div><b>S</b>ignal the missing belief<span>What would reduce that uncertainty?</span></div>
<div><b>T</b>ake one step<span>The smallest appropriate move. Then read the response and loop again.</span></div>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 10. The TRUST Loop. "Take one step" can be an answer, an offer, a pause or a respectful close.</p>

On a routine exchange, the loop takes seconds. On an important or confusing thread, slow it down with the long form: the **Trust Debugger**.

## The Trust Debugger

The Debugger is the TRUST Loop with the zoom turned up: thirteen questions for when a thread matters or has stalled. (Appendix A has the printable version. The AI Thread Debugger in the Implementation System runs these same questions on a pasted thread.)

1. What does the buyer believe right now?
2. What don't they believe yet?
3. What are they protecting?
4. Which gate are they at?
5. What is their emotional posture? (Keep an alternative reading.)
6. What's the state of P(outcome)?
7. What value is actually relevant to them?
8. What perceived risk, or hard constraint, exists?
9. What was the last trust deposit?
10. What was the last trust withdrawal?
11. What signal is missing?
12. What is the smallest move that could create it?
13. **What should I NOT do?**

Question 13 is the one most people skip, and it's often the most valuable. Naming the tempting wrong move ("send another testimonial," "ask for a call," "offer a discount") is the fastest way to avoid it.

## Action: assign a job before editing

Take three messages you're about to send. For each:

1. Write the primary trust job in one line.
2. Write the evidence that this job matters now.
3. Delete every sentence that serves a different, unrequested job.
4. Keep any information needed for accuracy, even if it makes the message longer.

Then run the full Debugger on your single most important open thread, and send only what you can justify.

**Stop:** treating every touch as a complete sales presentation. **Start:** defining the question before choosing the asset. **Notice:** unrelated asks that creep in while you edit. **Measure:** whether someone else can read your message and name its purpose.

<div class="rule">Field rule: One touch. One trust job. Answer every material question honestly.</div>

# Interlude: Trust Is Not a Trick

You can now read buyer state, spot hesitation, name the risk someone is protecting and identify what they don't yet believe.

That's a real capability, and like any real capability it can be misused. Everything in this book can be turned into manipulation by someone who wants to: a Mirror that invents pain, an Insight Gift designed to create debt, "safety" performed rather than provided. So before we get to the conversation itself, the line needs to be explicit.

**The purpose of trust is not to bypass consent. It's to make the decision environment clearer and safer, so the buyer can decide well, including deciding no.**

## The ethical floor

Never:

- **Fabricate familiarity.** "Great catching up at the conference!" when you didn't.
- **Fake compliments.** Praise you don't mean, attached to a pitch.
- **Manufacture urgency.** Invented deadlines, fake "only two spots left."
- **Manipulate fear.** Inflating the cost of inaction to scare someone into moving.
- **Exploit vulnerability.** Turning a personal disclosure into sales leverage.
- **Lie about scarcity.** If capacity is limited, say so factually, with dates. If it isn't, don't imply it.
- **Fabricate proof.** Invented testimonials, results, case studies or client logos. Beyond trust, this is legal exposure: fake reviews and testimonials are regulated.
- **Create false social pressure.** "Everyone in your space is already doing this."
- **Fake personalization.** Presenting a templated message or offer as designed for them.
- **Keep contacting someone who has said no.** Not through a new channel, not through a colleague, not with a "one last thing."

## The Screenshot Test

Before sending, ask: *Would I be comfortable if this person screenshotted this thread and posted it with my name on it?*

If the answer is no, the message fails, however well it might "work."

## Why this is also the commercially smart position

This isn't only a moral argument. In a trust graph, manipulation compounds too. The buyer you pressured remembers. So do the three people they mention you to. A tactic that wins this week's deal and poisons next year's referrals is a bad trade, even if you only care about revenue.

Trust moves deals. Pressure breaks them, sometimes slowly enough that you don't notice until the pipeline is gone.
