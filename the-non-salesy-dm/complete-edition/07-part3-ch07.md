# Chapter 7: Starting Conversations Without Pitching

<p class="lede">Personalize the reason for speaking, not just the greeting.</p>

A marketing consultant sees a founder announce a new service and writes: *"Congrats on the launch! Are you struggling to generate leads?"*

The compliment is specific. The question isn't. Nothing in the announcement suggested a lead problem. The consultant took a real observation and bolted a generic pitch onto it.

This is **personalization theater**: surface detail that makes a generic message look tailored. It was already common, and now that AI tools can "personalize" a thousand messages an hour, it's everywhere. Buyers have learned to spot it instantly. In an inbox full of automated personalization, the scarce signal is **evidence that a human actually paid attention.**

## The personalization test

Remove the personal detail from your message. Does the question or offer still fit almost anyone? If yes, you've decorated the message, not personalized it.

Real personalization changes the **substance**. It works at three levels:

- **Situation:** a specific launch, role change, process or business decision.
- **Viewpoint:** an argument they actually made, including a thoughtful disagreement.
- **Work product:** a public offer, resource or workflow you actually examined.

Stay out of their personal life. A prospect should never wonder why a stranger researched their family, their holidays or their unrelated accounts. Relevant professional context is enough.

### Observation → Implication → Question

1. **Observation:** name something you actually saw.
2. **Implication:** connect it carefully to business, if the connection isn't obvious.
3. **Question:** invite them to confirm, reject or refine it.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** Your new service page lists a strategy sprint next to the retainer. I work on service positioning, and that creates an interesting buying choice. Are those meant for different client stages?
</div>

The observation is verifiable. The sender's relevance is visible. The question doesn't assume anything is broken. When the connection is already obvious, drop the implication sentence. Shorter is better when the meaning survives.

## Five entry modes

Each entry mode is a way of passing G1 (Reality) and G2 (Relevance). They communicate different things, and each has a line where it turns manipulative.

### 1. Context

A real shared circumstance: an actual exchange, an event, an introduction, a discussion you both took part in.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** We talked about proposal ownership in the workshop yesterday, and your point about decisions getting stuck with the founder stayed with me. Has your team changed who can approve revisions?
</div>

**Communicates:** continuity, not a cold pitch. **When it fits:** there's a genuine shared moment. **When it turns manipulative:** claiming a relationship because both names were on an attendee list.

### 2. Observation

Something they published or built, with a question about the relevant distinction, not an accusation of a fault.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** Your new page separates an advisory sprint from ongoing implementation. I work on service positioning. Are those aimed at different buying stages, or different buyers?
</div>

**Communicates:** you looked at their work. **When it fits:** the observation connects to your expertise. **When it turns manipulative:** presenting a public-page observation as proof they're losing revenue.

### 3. Shared interest

A substantive idea you both care about, with no commercial motion required. That is fine.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** Your argument that founders should stay close to customer interviews makes sense to me. Where do you draw the line between staying informed and becoming the bottleneck?
</div>

**Communicates:** a peer conversation. **When it fits:** you genuinely have a view. **When it turns manipulative:** faking agreement to open a door, or treating every discussion as disguised discovery.

### 4. Content bridge

Content they actually engaged with, meaning a comment or reply, not a like.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** You mentioned in the comments that your team gets inquiries for the wrong service. Is that mismatch already visible on the form, or does it only show up on the call?
</div>

**Communicates:** you remembered what they contributed. **When it fits:** they volunteered context publicly. **When it turns manipulative:** sending everyone who liked a post an unsolicited audit.

### 5. Permission-based curiosity

You have a relevant observation, but sharing it needs their attention. Ask first, specifically.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** I noticed one spot where your workshop description and booking page seem to promise different outputs. Would a short note on that be useful?
</div>

**Communicates:** a bounded choice. **When it fits:** the observation is real and worth a few minutes. **When it turns manipulative:** holding back an obvious one-line point just to force a reply, or turning the "short note" into a full unsolicited review.

## Start at the right level of familiarity

An existing client, someone who requested your resource and a stranger who posted about a problem shouldn't get the same opening. The relationship changes what needs explaining and what you can reasonably ask.

| Entry route | What's already true | How to open |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing relationship** | Reality and some Relevance | Use the shared history honestly; say plainly if you want to discuss your service. Don't disguise a pitch as a social catch-up. |
| **Requested resource** | Permission to receive that resource | Deliver it first, in the promised format. No surprise sales gate. A download isn't permission for a sequence. |
| **Explicit public request** | They asked for help | Be direct: "You asked for someone who can improve handoffs. That's work I do." Don't make them earn basic information. |
| **Real introduction** | Whatever the introducer actually said | Stay inside it. "Sam thought you might be open to talking" ≠ "Sam said you urgently need my help." |
| **New contact** | Nothing | A specific observation, your area of work, and one question that can be answered "no." |

**Connection requests** are small introductions, not compressed pitches. LinkedIn's policies restrict promotional invitations to people you don't know. Give a genuine reason to connect, or leave it blank. If you have no meaningful context, research more or don't send.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**After a real exchange:** Maya, enjoyed the discussion on your handoff post. I'd be glad to stay connected.

**After acceptance:** Thanks for connecting, Maya. In your handoff post you mentioned account managers inheriting unclear promises. I work on that process. Is the difficulty usually missing detail, or conflicting expectations?
</div>

If the answer is "Neither, we've sorted it," accept the correction. A relevant question has done its job even when it removes the opportunity. An accepted connection is access, not interest in your service.

**When they ask "What do you do?"** answer in buyer language and let them decide whether it connects:

> "I help small agencies fix the handoff between what sales promises and what delivery receives. The work is usually a shared brief, ownership rules and a pilot with one account team. It's most relevant when the founder keeps getting pulled into kickoff problems."

You don't need to end every message with a question. Sometimes a clear answer is the most useful thing you can give.

## Don't overproduce the first touch

A two-page teardown, an unsolicited video or a custom strategy in the first message feels like a demand for attention, however generous you meant it. Start with the most relevant observation. If they want more, ask what format helps and deliver something short and bounded.

The exception: when someone explicitly asks for examples, send them. The governing principle is the recipient's context, not a blanket ban on links.

## Before the first DM: what the profile must confirm

Remember the 3Cs. Your opener creates a question ("who is this?"), and the buyer will usually answer it by clicking your profile. Before a new outreach block, check:

- Does my headline match the topic of my openers?
- Would someone arriving from this DM find evidence for the area of work I mentioned?
- Is there one clear place to go next (a sample, a service explanation, a way to reach me)?

If your opener says "I work on agency handoffs" and your profile says "Growth Hacker | Web3 | Coach," you've failed G1 before the buyer reads your second sentence.

## Action: the swap test

1. Draft openers for five real people, one for each entry mode.
2. Label each with the gate it serves (almost always G1 or G2) and the entry route.
3. **Swap the names.** If the messages still fit, your research didn't change the substance. Revise.
4. For each, write the reply you'd send if they said "that's not a problem for us."
5. Run the Pause Test. Send at most two today.

**Stop:** using compliments as camouflage. **Start:** grounding every question in a verifiable observation. **Notice:** when an opener assumes a problem nobody reported. **Measure:** the share of your openers you could defend with a specific source.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Personalization is evidence of attention. It can't be faked for long.</div>
