# Chapter 8: The Five Trust Moves

<p class="lede">Pick the move from the missing signal, not from your habits.</p>

A buyer says: *"We tried this last year."*

You could sympathize. You could explain your method. You could offer a useful reframe. You could tell a story. You could admit a limitation in your experience. All five are legitimate moves. Only one or two fit what this sentence means *in this conversation*.

The Five Trust Moves are the vocabulary for the last step of the TRUST Loop, "Take one step." They aren't a sequence to perform in order. They're the options, and choosing well is the skill.

<div class="moves">
<div><b>Mirror</b><span>Reflect what they're experiencing</span></div>
<div><b>Clarifier</b><span>Make something simple and transparent</span></div>
<div><b>Insight Gift</b><span>Offer a useful reframe, no strings</span></div>
<div><b>Story</b><span>Ground the abstract in the concrete</span></div>
<div><b>Self-Disclosure</b><span>Share a small, honest truth</span></div>

## Move 1 · Mirror

**What it is:** Reflecting the *meaning* of what the buyer has expressed, accurately enough that they can confirm or correct it.

**Purpose:** "You don't need to defend your hesitation here." An accurate reflection removes the need to repeat or justify the point, and it makes your understanding inspectable.

**Use when:** the buyer is hesitant, feels misunderstood, needs emotional safety, or resistance is present. It's especially strong at **G3 (Fit)** and **G6 (Identity)**.

<div class="sidebar" markdown="1">
**Not the same as "mirroring."** If you've read Chris Voss's *Never Split the Difference*, you know "mirroring" as a negotiation technique: repeating the last few words someone said to prompt them to elaborate. That's a useful tool, but it isn't this. The Mirror in this book reflects **the buyer's experience and meaning**, not their syntax. A Voss mirror of "we tried this last year" is "Tried it last year?" A trust Mirror is "So you've already spent time on this once, and it didn't give you what you needed."
</div>

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** I don't want another agency that needs me to rewrite everything.

**Rep:** The concern is that outsourcing just creates a second editing job for you.

**Buyer:** Exactly. I want to spend less time on it, not just move the work around.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Test the meaning of the concern |
| **Buyer may experience** | Understood without being argued with |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G3 understanding plus G4 workload risk |
| **Better move** | Confirm the desired reduction in their involvement *before* describing your process |
| **Why** | "We save you time" isn't an answer until you know where their time went last time |
</div>

**Misuse:** inventing emotion ("That must be devastating" about an inconvenience), exaggerating pain, repeating words to *simulate* listening, or tacking "…which is exactly why you need us" onto every reflection.

**Practice:** Take three buyer statements. Paraphrase the substantive meaning of each in one sentence. Remove any emotion the buyer didn't express. Add "Have I got that right?" only where you're genuinely unsure, not as a verbal tic.

## Move 2 · Clarifier

**What it is:** Explaining a process, distinction, claim or commitment in plain terms.

**Purpose:** Reduce ambiguity. The buyer can't evaluate a next step they don't understand.

**Use when:** uncertainty exists, the process is unclear, friction is unclear, or the buyer is worried about what happens next. It's the workhorse of **G1, G2, G4 and G5**.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** What do you mean by a positioning sprint?

**Consultant:** We pick one buyer situation, look at how your current offer is described, and produce a message hierarchy your team can actually use. Two working sessions and a written summary. It doesn't include rewriting the whole website.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Make the offer concrete |
| **Buyer may experience** | The work and its boundary can be judged |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G2 relevance and G5 value can't be assessed while the term is vague |
| **Better move** | Outputs, participation and exclusions, in that order |
| **Why** | Operational detail beats a branded label |
</div>

**Misuse:** a lecture; more jargon than the original statement; the important limitation buried in the last sentence of a long message. Put material boundaries where the buyer will actually see them.

**Practice:** Explain your offer to someone outside your industry in three sentences: what happens, what they receive, what's excluded. Have them explain it back. Revise every part they had to guess.

---

## Move 3 · Insight Gift

**What it is:** A useful observation, reframe or small map, given without asking for anything back.

**Purpose:** Show how you think, and improve the buyer's thinking, without creating obligation.

**Use when:** curiosity exists, the buyer is resistant to being controlled or sold to, they need evidence that you can think, and you can genuinely add clarity. Strong at **G2** and **G3**.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Founder:** We get plenty of calls, but they rarely go anywhere.

**Rep:** One thing worth checking: do people understand the service before they book? If the invitation promises advice and the call evaluates a paid engagement, the mismatch may start before the call does.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Offer a useful hypothesis |
| **Buyer may experience** | A concrete angle to check, no strings attached |
| **Trust diagnosis** | The issue isn't understood yet; the insight narrows the investigation |
| **Better move** | Offer the distinction without declaring it *the* cause |
| **Why** | A good hypothesis improves their thinking without overclaiming |
</div>

**The test:** an Insight Gift must remain useful **even if they never buy from you.** Delete the call-to-action. If what's left has no standalone value, it wasn't a gift.

**Misuse:** the baited gift ("I found seven expensive mistakes on your site, book a call to see them"); excessive free work that creates pressure or resentment; a full "free audit" that was really a disguised pitch. An Insight Gift is a *reframe*, not a deliverable. Free consulting is a different decision, and Chapter 11 covers where to draw that line.

**Practice:** Write a two-sentence insight based on a public business artifact. Label the observation and the hypothesis. Remove the ask. Improve it until it stands on its own.

## Move 4 · Story

**What it is:** A short narrative, analogy or example that makes an abstract mechanism concrete.

**Purpose:** Resonance. It helps the buyer see themselves in the situation and understand *how* the change would work.

**Use when:** the buyer needs to picture it, abstraction needs grounding, or you're explaining a trade-off. Strong at **G5 (Value)**.

A useful story has four parts: a **starting condition**, a **choice or intervention**, **what changed**, and a **limitation**. The limitation is what makes it believable.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** I don't see why changing a handoff document would matter.

**Consultant:** Picture this: sales agrees to weekly reporting, but delivery's brief says monthly. The kickoff meeting now has to reconstruct the sale. A shared brief fixes that only if the commitment gets recorded *before* the handoff and someone checks it. The document alone isn't the whole solution.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Make the mechanism visible |
| **Buyer may experience** | The change becomes easy to evaluate |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G5: how does the intervention create value? |
| **Better move** | Show the dependency on process and ownership |
| **Why** | The example explains instead of just impressing |
</div>

**Misuse:** presenting an illustration as a client story; choosing a case with a dramatic result while hiding that the starting conditions were completely different; treating a precedent as a guarantee. **Never fabricate a testimonial or outcome.** If it's hypothetical, say "picture this" or "as an illustration." If it's real, you need permission to identify anyone.

**Practice:** Write one story, true or clearly labeled as illustrative, in four sentences: starting condition, mechanism, outcome, limitation. Delete every detail that exists only to inflate your authority.

---

## Move 5 · Self-Disclosure

**What it is:** A small, relevant, honest truth about your perspective, experience, limitation or process.

**Purpose:** Break through polished sales language. Skepticism is often caused by messages that sound too perfect. A real limitation makes everything else you say more believable.

**Use when:** skepticism comes from performative language, transparency would reduce uncertainty, or you can acknowledge an imperfect reality. Strong at **G1** and **G3**.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** Have you done this for our exact sector?

**Consultant:** Not your exact sector. My relevant work is the same proposal-to-delivery problem in project-based service firms. I can show you that process, but we'd need to validate the sector-specific parts together. If exact-sector experience is essential for you, I might not be the right fit.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | State the experience boundary |
| **Buyer may experience** | A decision based on accurate information |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G3 fit and P(outcome) are partly uncertain, and now honestly so |
| **Better move** | Say what transfers and what doesn't |
| **Why** | An honest limitation beats an inflated credential, and it may lose a deal you shouldn't win |
</div>

Here's a disclosure from my own outbound that I'd use in a conversation with a B2B founder: *"We had a 30% reply rate and it still wasn't working, because replies weren't turning into bookings. So I don't judge outbound by reply rate anymore."* It's specific, slightly unflattering and directly relevant. That's what makes it work.

**Misuse:** oversharing personal difficulties, invoking financial need, performing vulnerability to create obligation, or expecting the buyer to reward your honesty with a purchase. The disclosure should help *them* evaluate, not take care of your feelings.

**Practice:** Identify one limitation buyers should know about before committing. State it plainly, give its practical implication, and explain how you'd handle it if the engagement goes ahead.

## Choosing the move

<div class="table-caption">Five Trust Moves Matrix</div>

| If the missing signal is… | First move to consider | Common wrong move |
|---|---|---|
| "They haven't understood me." | **Mirror** | More proof, or a faster pitch |
| "I don't know what this means." | **Clarifier** | More terminology |
| "I need a useful way to think about this." | **Insight Gift** | A huge unrequested audit |
| "I can't picture how it would work." | **Story** | An unrelated success claim |
| "This sounds too polished to trust." | **Self-Disclosure** | Manufactured vulnerability |

The matrix is a starting point. Sometimes the right move is none of the five: a direct factual answer, a pause, a referral, or a clear no.

### Combining without overloading

Two moves can serve one job. A Mirror followed by a Clarifier is natural: acknowledge the concern, then explain the relevant process. Five moves stacked into one reply to a simple question is the overloaded DM again, just with better vocabulary.

After any move, read what changed. Did they correct you? Ask something more specific? Raise a new concern? Decline? Your next move answers that, not your plan.

## Action: the move log

For one week, tag every DM you send with its move (or "direct answer," "pause," "offer"). At the end of the week, look at the distribution.

Most people discover they overuse one move (usually the Insight Gift, because it feels generous) and almost never use another (usually Self-Disclosure, because it feels risky). Deliberately practice your least-used move next week.

**Stop:** choosing the move you enjoy most. **Start:** choosing the move that supplies the missing signal. **Notice:** when "help" creates an implied debt. **Measure:** whether the buyer's actual uncertainty got clearer, not whether they praised your message.

<div class="rule">Field rule: A move is only useful when it fits the missing signal.</div>
