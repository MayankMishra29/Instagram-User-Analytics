# Chapter 9: Discovering the Problem Without Interrogating the Buyer

<p class="lede">Don't interrogate someone into vulnerability. Create enough safety that they volunteer context.</p>

A founder mentions that proposals are taking longer than they used to. The seller immediately asks for monthly revenue, team size, close rate, budget and the name of the decision-maker.

The founder answers none of them.

Every one of those questions might eventually matter. Arriving as a bundle, they turn a conversation into an intake form the buyer never agreed to fill out. The seller is extracting qualification data before showing how that information helps the *buyer*.

## Extraction vs. conversation

| **Extraction-based discovery** | **Conversation-based discovery** |
|---|---|
| "What's your biggest challenge?" | "What happened with the last delayed proposal?" |
| The seller collects data to decide whether to pitch | Both sides investigate whether there's a problem worth solving |
| Many questions per message | About one real question per message, tied to the last answer |
| The buyer does the work | The seller gives something (a reflection, a distinction) before asking |
| Asks for vulnerability before Safety | Earns context by being useful |

"What's your biggest challenge?" is the classic failure. It asks a stranger to do an unbounded business review for someone they don't trust yet. In gate terms, it's a G6-level ask (share your real problems) made at G2.

## The acknowledge → reflect → ask loop

1. **Acknowledge** the substance of what they said.
2. **Reflect** what you think you heard, when it tests a meaningful interpretation.
3. **Ask** one question that resolves the most important uncertainty.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** We get enough inquiries, but half aren't a fit.

**Rep:** So volume's fine, and the mismatch is the issue. What usually makes an inquiry unsuitable?
</div>

The question follows the buyer's own language. It doesn't jump to budget, headcount or a meeting. If they say "too small," you can ask what minimum situation makes their service useful. If they say "wrong service," you can explore what's attracting them. **Different answers should lead to different conversations**, and that's how you know you're discovering rather than running a script.

Skip robotic reflection. You don't need "It sounds like…" before every sentence. Reflect when it tests something important, especially before you recommend anything.

## The question budget

Every conversation has a **question budget**: the number of questions it can bear at its current trust level. Early on, it's about one per message, and ideally you've given something first (a relevant observation, a useful distinction, a straight answer to their question). As trust grows, the budget grows. Buyers at G5 and G6 often *want* you to ask detailed questions, because it shows you're taking their situation seriously.

When you blow the budget, the symptoms are predictable: shorter replies, answers to only the easiest question, then silence.

## What natural discovery reveals

When discovery works, the buyer's own account surfaces seven things, usually in whatever order *they* choose:

| Element | Question that invites it naturally |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | "What happened in a recent example?" |
| **Consequence** | "What did that affect, if anything important?" |
| **Desire** | "What would you like to be different?" |
| **Previous attempts** | "What's been tried so far?" |
| **Hesitation** | "What would make a change hard to pull off right now?" |
| **Urgency** | "Is this something you want to tackle now, or later?" |
| **Constraints** | "What would make a fix practical or impractical for your team?" |

These are a reference, not a questionnaire. You won't need all seven in the inbox. Stop when you have enough to choose the next step.

### Start with a recent example

Concrete beats abstract every time. "What happened with the last unsuitable inquiry?" produces far better information than "What's your biggest pain point?" It also stops you from building a diagnosis around an abstract label.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Founder:** Proposals are taking too long.

**Consultant:** What held up the most recent one?

**Founder:** I had to approve the scope, but I was traveling.

**Consultant:** Was the team missing information to decide, or did it just need your sign-off?

**Founder:** Mostly sign-off. They knew what to recommend.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **What the rep thinks they're doing** | Separating an information delay from an authority delay |
| **Buyer may experience** | The conversation is clarifying their problem, not collecting unrelated facts |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G3: the real bottleneck is approval rights, not proposal writing |
| **Better move** | Explore the approval rule before recommending a proposal tool |
| **Why** | Automating document production wouldn't remove the founder dependency |
</div>

### Discover consequence without amplifying pain

Ask what the problem affects. It might cost momentum, disrupt scheduling, create rework, or just be annoying. The buyer may not know, and "unknown" beats a dramatic estimate.

**Quantify only what the buyer can support.** If a founder says the team takes ten unsuitable calls a month at about 30 minutes each, that supports roughly five hours in those calls. It does *not* support "you're losing $20,000 a month." Preparation and follow-up may add time, so ask before including them:

> "Ten calls at about half an hour is roughly five hours in the calls themselves. Is there meaningful prep or follow-up on top of that?"

The useful question is "What changes for the business when this happens?" The intrusive version is "How much money are you losing every day by not fixing this?" The second assumes a loss and carries a judgment before any facts exist.

### Discover desire without supplying the answer

A founder may want fewer interruptions, not faster proposals. An agency may want more predictable delivery, not more clients. A creator may want to protect their voice more than to publish more often. Repeat the objective accurately, and if it's outside your service, say so. Don't translate every goal into the outcome you happen to sell.

### Learn from previous attempts

A failed attempt is evidence about conditions, not proof that your method is better. Ask what changed, what didn't, and how they judged success. A previous provider may have delivered competently while the internal owner had no capacity. Don't attack the incumbent, and don't fish for confidential details about another provider. You need the mechanism, not the gossip.

## Give value without taking over

Once you understand a concrete issue, offer **one bounded insight**:

> "If the mismatch is usually company stage, it might be worth testing one explicit suitability line before the booking link. That's a small experiment before redesigning the whole funnel."

Useful even if no sale follows. Not a full consulting plan in the thread. Boundaries protect both sides: the buyer gets something they can assess, and you avoid speculative work based on half the context.

## Handling short and ambiguous replies

| Reply | What to do |
|---|---|
| **"Yes, exactly."** | Summarize the problem and ask the next important question. Agreement with your description isn't agreement to buy. |
| **"Maybe."** | Reduce the ambiguity: "Is the uncertainty about whether it's a problem, or whether now's the time?" If it stays vague, stop pushing for a decision. |
| **"Interesting."** | Make it easy to clarify or exit: "Happy to leave it there. If you're actively looking at this, I can explain what the work involves." |
| **A long, detailed answer** | Respond to the main concern first. If it's getting unwieldy, offer a choice: "There are a few connected pieces here. Would a short call help, or would you rather keep it in messages?" |

## When the buyer doesn't want discovery

Sometimes a person just wants a straightforward answer about a standard service. Give it. Don't respond to "What does the workshop cost?" with five questions designed to make them disclose a problem first. That's the hiding failure mode wearing a discovery costume.

And sometimes a person is happy to discuss an idea but not their business. Respect that boundary. A professional conversation can stay collegial without becoming an opportunity.

## Qualification belongs inside the conversation

You still need to know whether the work fits, whether the buyer cares, whether it's feasible and how a decision gets made. Qualification serves the buyer as well as you: it stops them investing time in something that can't solve their problem. The difference is that you **explain why a detail matters** before asking:

> "The workshop needs both offer owners in the room, so I want to check whether they can both make it."

That's far better than asking "Who's the decision-maker?" as if the person you're talking to were an obstacle. Chapter 11 covers price and authority in detail.

## Action: the problem map

For one live thread, fill in the seven elements: problem, consequence, desire, previous attempts, hesitation, urgency and constraints. Mark each one:

- **Buyer-stated**: they told you.
- **Inferred**: you're guessing.
- **Unknown**.

Pick the single unknown that could change your recommendation, and write a proportionate question about it.

Then audit five recent discovery threads. Count the questions you asked versus the context the buyer volunteered. **Volunteered context is the best leading indicator of trust you have.** If you're asking far more than they're offering, you're extracting.

**Stop:** asking every qualification question at once. **Start:** using the last answer to choose the next question. **Notice:** when the buyer's goal differs from what your offer promises. **Measure:** more accurate summaries, fewer corrected assumptions, fewer proposals built on guessed problems.
