# Chapter 3: The Trust Utility Equation

<p class="lede">Don't add value to compensate for risk. Remove the risk.</p>

Amir sells a workflow implementation service. A prospect writes: *"I get what you do. I'm just not sure it's worth taking on right now."*

Amir sends another testimonial. The prospect gets quieter.

The testimonial would have helped if the prospect doubted Amir could deliver. They didn't. They were worried about something else entirely: the hours their team would have to put into implementation. More proof of capability does nothing for a capacity problem. It actually makes things worse, because it signals Amir isn't listening.

The mistake is treating hesitation as one problem with one remedy. It's usually one of three problems, and each needs a different response.

## The model

<div class="equation">
<div class="eq-main">Trust Utility = <span>P(outcome)</span> × <span>Value</span> − <span>Perceived Risk</span></div>
<div class="eq-parts">
<div><b>P(outcome)</b><br>"Can this actually work <i>for me</i>?"</div>
<div><b>Value</b><br>"Does this change matter to <i>me</i>, now?"</div>
<div><b>Perceived Risk</b><br>"What could I lose, or have to put in?"</div>
</div>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 5. Trust Utility. Find the weak term, and send the signal that strengthens that term, not the asset you happen to have.</p>

- **P(outcome)** is how plausible the desired result feels to *this* buyer in *this* situation. Not in general. For them.
- **Value** is how much that result matters to them, compared with everything else they could spend time and money on.
- **Perceived Risk** is everything they believe they could lose or have to endure by moving forward.

When a buyer won't move, one of these terms is usually weak. Your job is to find which one, and to stop throwing effort at the others.

### How to use it (and how not to)

This is a decision model for diagnosing a stalled conversation, not a calculator. The terms don't share units, and you should never write "P(outcome) = 0.7" about someone who hasn't told you anything that precise. Instead, rate each term in plain language:

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | The buyer has said or shown this directly |
| **Weak** | The buyer has expressed doubt about this |
| **Unknown** | You haven't heard enough to say |

An "unknown" is the most useful finding in the whole exercise. It tells you what to ask next.

<div class="sidebar" markdown="1">
**If this looks familiar**

If you've read Alex Hormozi's *$100M Offers*, you'll see a family resemblance to his value equation (dream outcome × perceived likelihood ÷ time delay × effort and sacrifice). You may also know Maister, Green and Galford's trust equation from *The Trusted Advisor*, which scores an advisor's credibility, reliability and intimacy against their self-orientation.

Both are excellent, and they answer different questions. Hormozi's equation helps you **design an offer**, which is mostly a static exercise. Maister's helps you assess **how trustworthy an advisor is**. Trust Utility diagnoses **a specific buyer's decision at a specific moment in a specific conversation**, and it changes with every message you send. Hormozi helps you build the offer. Trust Utility tells you whether *this* message should mention it.
</div>

## P(outcome): believable for this buyer

General expertise is not the same as a believable outcome in a specific setting. A case study from a large software company proves you've done serious work, and it may leave a two-person consultancy unsure your approach fits their resources.

Ask what makes the outcome uncertain for them:

- Is the service unclear?
- Is the method poorly explained?
- Is your evidence from a different context?
- Did a previous attempt fail?
- Does success depend on things they can't do?

The right signal follows the gap. A short explanation fixes an unclear method. A relevant work sample makes delivery concrete. A bounded pilot tests a real uncertainty. And an honest statement about the limits of your experience helps the buyer decide accurately, even if it lowers the chance of a sale. **Proof has to stay true.** Never invent a matching case study because the buyer wants one. Explain what transfers and what still needs validating.

## Value: important to them, not impressive to you

An outcome can be desirable in principle and irrelevant right now. "More leads" means little to a firm that can't deliver its current work. "Save five hours a week" is nice, but the buyer may care far more about fewer errors, a protected client relationship or better visibility.

Don't substitute your favorite metric for their objective. Ask what the change would make possible, and accept that the answer might reveal low priority.

A coach says their newsletter takes too long. You sell a content system. Before you put a dollar figure on anything, ask what they'd do with the time. Maybe client delivery. Maybe just fewer late nights. Both matter. Neither automatically becomes revenue, so don't pretend it does.

Value also includes fit. A useful service is still the wrong choice if a simpler process, a cheaper tool or doing nothing would be enough. Saying so out loud is one of the most trust-building sentences in selling.

## Perceived risk: much more than the fee

Buyers protect themselves from far more than financial loss:

<div class="risk-grid">
<span>Financial</span><span>Time</span><span>Implementation workload</span><span>Opportunity cost</span><span>Credibility with colleagues</span><span>Identity ("is this who we are?")</span><span>Emotional (fear of being fooled again)</span><span>Social (what will my team think?)</span><span>Complexity</span><span>Process uncertainty</span><span>Previous bad experience</span><span>Friction</span>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 6. The risk taxonomy. Price is usually the most visible risk and rarely the only one.</p>

| What the buyer says | What to clarify | Signal that may help |
|---|---|---|
| "My team is already overloaded." | Required participation and timing | Honest workload, dependencies, or a later start |
| "The last provider disappeared." | Communication and ownership | A named owner, a review rhythm, clear remedies |
| "I have to justify this internally." | Their evaluation criteria and approval process | A short, factual decision summary they can forward |
| "I'm not sure what happens next." | Process and commitment | A short sequence of steps with explicit boundaries |
| "We can't afford that." | Whether the budget is truly fixed | A smaller scope that still works, a pause, or a no |

Reducing perceived risk doesn't mean assuring the buyer that risk has vanished. It means making the situation clearer and, where possible, **changing the engagement** to reduce real exposure. Being transparent about a genuine limitation may make the buyer more aware of risk at first. That beats a sale built on concealed information, which becomes a refund, a bad review or a lost referral later.

## The Trust Tax

Every unnecessary bit of friction you add raises perceived risk. That's the **trust tax**:

- A vague offer
- A confusing process
- Too many links
- A wall of text
- A sudden pitch
- An unexpected call request
- An exaggerated promise
- An unclear next step

Each one hands the buyer a new small uncertainty to resolve. Your job is to **reduce unnecessary uncertainty without manufacturing false certainty.** This is also why "adding value" can lower Trust Utility: the value term goes up a little, while the complexity and obligation terms of risk go up a lot.

## Diagnose before intervening

Back to Amir. Instead of another testimonial:

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Amir:** When you say it may not be worth taking on right now, is the doubt mainly about the result, your team's workload, or something else?

**Buyer:** Workload. We can't put three people into workshops this month.

**Amir:** That's a real constraint. The current scope does need those sessions, and I wouldn't promise the same result without them. We could plan a later start. Would next month be realistic?
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Amir's aim** | Find the actual reason for hesitation |
| **Buyer may experience** | A concrete constraint taken seriously, not argued away |
| **Trust diagnosis** | P(outcome): strong. Value: strong. **Risk: weak** (implementation capacity). More proof won't help |
| **Better move** | Clarify the requirement and move the timing |
| **Why** | No explanation can make unavailable capacity appear |
</div>

Notice what Amir *didn't* do. He didn't discount before understanding the issue. He didn't insist the team could "find the time." He didn't quietly remove the workshops and promise the same outcome.

## Four common diagnostic errors

1. **Low belief mistaken for low value.** They want the result and doubt you can deliver it. Repeating the benefits feels evasive. Address the evidence, the method or the fit.
2. **Low priority mistaken for fear.** They understand the offer and simply have more important work. Don't invent an emotional block to explain a rational choice.
3. **A hard constraint mistaken for a trust gap.** A budget freeze or an unavailable team stops a purchase even when confidence is high. Respect reality.
4. **Friendliness mistaken for utility.** Someone can enjoy the conversation without needing the service. Warmth and commercial fit are different questions.

## A worked diagnostic

A creator is evaluating a newsletter ghostwriting service. They believe the writer is skilled. They value consistent publishing. What worries them is that the writing will flatten their voice and make them sound generic.

**Record it like this:** "P(outcome): writing skill *strong*, voice preservation *unknown*. Value: *strong*, publishing consistency matters. Risk: *weak*, loss of voice plus review burden. Next move: explain the voice-review process and ask whether a short sample from their own material would help."

The missing signal isn't another list of newsletter benefits. It's evidence about the thing they're actually afraid of losing.

## The Compounding Loop

Trust Utility isn't static. It changes with every interaction, and when interactions are good, it compounds:

<div class="flow">
<span>Small useful interaction</span><span>Buyer feels understood</span><span>Less uncertainty</span><span>More openness</span><span>Better context</span><span>Sharper relevance</span><span>Higher P(outcome), lower risk</span><span>Voluntary movement</span>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 7. The Compounding Loop, the mirror image of the Decay Loop in Chapter 1.</p>

Each accurate, useful move gets you better information, and better information makes your next move more relevant. That's why trust-led conversations speed up in the middle while pressure-led ones slow down. It works in both directions, though: inconsistent claims compound confusion just as reliably.

## Action: the TU Diagnostic

Pick five stalled conversations. For each:

1. Rate **P(outcome)**, **Value** and **Perceived Risk** as *strong*, *weak* or *unknown*, and write the evidence behind each rating.
2. Circle the weakest term (or the most important unknown).
3. Write the one sentence that would move that term. **Don't send it yet.**
4. Run a counterfactual: *if the buyer already trusts my competence but has no budget, does my move still make sense?* If not, clarify the constraint first.

**Stop:** answering every hesitation with proof. **Start:** separating belief, value and risk. **Notice:** when the concern is about participation, not persuasion. **Measure:** whether your response addresses the concern the buyer confirms.

<div class="rule">Field rule: The missing signal depends on the uncertainty, not on the asset you want to send.</div>
