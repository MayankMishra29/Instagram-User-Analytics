# Chapter 10: Interest Is Not Readiness

<p class="lede">Interest tells you the subject matters. Readiness tells you a decision can happen.</p>

A consultant gets a long, thoughtful reply about a challenge the buyer is facing. She marks the opportunity "hot." On the call she learns the buyer is researching for *next year*, has no budget and no mandate.

The reply was substantive. Her readiness judgment wasn't.

This is the single most expensive misreading in outbound, and it's exactly the gap I lived through: a 30% reply rate that didn't turn into bookings. Replies are interest. Bookings require readiness. The distance between them is where this chapter lives.

## The Interest Ladder

<div class="ladder">
<div><b>Readiness</b><span>"We have approval. What do you need to start?"</span></div>
<div><b>Intent</b><span>"Can you send scope and pricing?"</span></div>
<div><b>Exploration</b><span>"What would you recommend for our situation?"</span></div>
<div><b>Interest</b><span>"How would that actually work?"</span></div>
<div><b>Curiosity</b><span>"Interesting."</span></div>
<div><b>Attention</b><span>A view, a like, a follow</span></div>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 12. The Interest Ladder. Each rung needs a different response, and people move down as well as up.</p>

| Signal | What it supports | What it doesn't establish |
|---|---|---|
| A view or like | Possible exposure or topic interest | A problem, or permission to contact repeatedly |
| "Interesting." | Acknowledgment or curiosity | Evaluation |
| "How would that work?" | Interest in the mechanism | A current project or budget |
| "What would you recommend for us?" | An invitation to discuss fit | Agreement to your scope |
| "Can you send scope and price?" | Active evaluation | Authorized approval |
| "We have approval. What's needed to start?" | Strong readiness | Completed agreement and payment |

The ladder protects you in both directions. **When the buyer is only curious, don't act as if a decision is overdue.** When they're ready, stop giving them introductory education they didn't ask for. That's the hiding failure mode, and it loses deals that should have closed.

## Readiness has practical conditions

Wanting the result isn't readiness. Before proposing substantial work, look for evidence of:

1. **An acknowledged problem**, in their words
2. **A meaningful desired change**
3. **A reason to act now** (or a named time)
4. **A feasible engagement**: time, people, participation
5. **A real decision path**: who evaluates and who approves

A buyer may want the result and lack the time or authority. Another may have resources but no priority. A team may agree on the solution and disagree on who owns it. **None of these are trust failures.** They're readiness conditions, and pushing on them makes things worse.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Buyer:** This is interesting. We may need it at some point.

**Rep:** Happy to explain it. Are you exploring a current project, or mostly collecting ideas for later?

**Buyer:** Later. We're hiring an operations lead first.

**Rep:** Makes sense. Whoever owns the process would matter a lot for implementation. I'll leave you with the overview. No need to evaluate anything now.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Lens | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Rep's aim** | Clarify timing without manufacturing urgency |
| **Buyer may experience** | Curiosity is allowed without an immediate sales obligation |
| **Trust diagnosis** | G2 relevance: yes. Readiness: blocked by an unresolved ownership condition |
| **Better move** | Give the overview; stop active evaluation |
| **Why** | A premature proposal creates work the buyer can't use yet, and a memory of being pushed |
</div>

Notice what's missing: "I'll follow up every month." If a later revisit would help, ask about a specific **trigger** ("Would it be useful if I checked back once the ops lead is in place?") and accept the answer.

## Signal Lag

Here's the counterweight. A person can read your content for months, look at your profile twice, remember one idea you shared, and never tell you. Then one day they write: *"Do you still run that workshop?"*

That's **Signal Lag**: the delay between trust forming and trust showing up as action.

<div class="example" markdown="1">
**Illustrative.** Kofi runs a newsletter and paid workshops. One subscriber likes six of his posts over five weeks and never replies to the DM Kofi sent after the second like. Kofi assumes a dead lead. In week seven, the subscriber writes: "Do you still run the positioning workshop? Our team needs it before Q1." Nothing Kofi sent in weeks three to six caused that message. The content did the work, the profile confirmed it, and the buyer moved when *their* conditions were met.
</div>

Signal Lag teaches two things at once:

1. **No immediate action doesn't mean no trust.** Many buyers are building memory quietly.
2. **Signal Lag is not proof of hidden intent.** A profile view can mean curiosity, a misclick or a hiring check. Don't use the idea to turn every silent person into a secretly interested prospect you're entitled to chase.

## The four kinds of quiet

When a thread goes quiet, it's usually one of these:

| Type | What it looks like | Right response |
|---|---|---|
| **Dormant trust** | Real prior engagement, then quiet; they may still be consuming your content | Leave it. Rehydrate when a relevant trigger appears (Chapter 14) |
| **Polite interest** | Courteous replies, no substance, no questions | Answer what they asked, then let it rest |
| **Active exploration** | Questions about their situation, then a pause (often while they evaluate internally) | One proportionate check-in tied to what's open |
| **Disengagement** | A clear decline, or repeated non-response with no active next step | Close the record |

## Handling silence correctly

Silence can mean busy, uncertain, uninterested, overwhelmed, distracted, evaluating, protecting themselves or simply forgetting. **Never interpret silence with certainty.** Make decisions that hold up under uncertainty.

Your options are:

- **Pause.** Often the best move. Nothing you could send would help right now.
- **Rehydrate.** Return later with context, if there's a legitimate reason.
- **Add context.** Clarify something genuinely ambiguous in your last message.
- **Offer a relevant insight.** Only if it's new and useful on its own, not "value" manufactured to create a touchpoint.
- **Close the loop.** A respectful status question or a close-out.
- **Move on.** Some conversations are finished.

**Not every silent conversation needs another message.**

### A working default for follow-up

These are conservative defaults, not rules, and a refusal always overrides the schedule:

| Situation | Default |
|---|---|
| **New contact, no reply** | After ~5–7 business days, one short follow-up that adds something (a clarification or a genuinely new observation) is reasonable. If still nothing, stop. |
| **Active conversation, then silence** | After ~3–5 business days, recap the open point and offer an easy way to pause. If appropriate, one close-out about a week later. Then stop. |
| **Agreed decision date** | Follow the date you agreed. If it passes, ask whether the timeline changed. |
| **"Come back in November"** | Confirm what you'll revisit and when, then wait. November is not permission for weekly reminders until then. |
| **Explicit no** | Acknowledge once if useful, record it and stop. No new channel, no colleague, no "one last thing." |

A follow-up that names the actual open loop works because it asks for a status, not an explanation:

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** We were looking at whether unsuitable inquiries come from the offer page or the booking form. Still something you want to work through, or should we leave it for now?
</div>

And a close-out, when it's time:

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Rep:** I'll close this out on my side for now. If the handoff issue becomes a priority, you're welcome to pick the thread back up.
</div>

Then actually close it. Don't follow a "final message" with three more final messages. Never use guilt ("I guess growth isn't a priority for you?"). That's an insult dressed as a question, and it burns the node in your graph.

## "I need to think about it"

This one phrase covers at least seven different situations:

- Low **P(outcome)**: they aren't sure it will work for them
- High **perceived risk**
- **Missing information**
- **Internal disagreement**
- A real **timing** issue
- **No urgency**
- A genuine wish to **think**

It can also be a polite no. Don't pick the interpretation most favorable to your sale and treat it as fact. **Diagnose before intervening.** One gentle clarification is enough:

> "Of course. Is there an open question I can help with, or would you rather have some time with it?"

If they name a concern, address that concern. If they want time, agree whether and when to reconnect. If they decline, stop. The goal isn't to prevent every delay. It's to keep an active decision separate from an indefinite possibility in your own records.

## Readiness can reverse

Projects lose sponsors. Budgets change. Delivery demand spikes. A buyer's earlier enthusiasm doesn't obligate them to proceed under different circumstances. When context changes, update the opportunity, and never use their earlier words as a trap ("But you said this was important"). Ask whether the priority has changed, without implying a moral failure.

## Action: the readiness check

Take the three opportunities you think are most likely to close. For each, write the evidence for:

1. Priority
2. Practical feasibility
3. Decision ownership
4. An agreed next step

If the record depends on phrases like "seemed excited" or "really liked the idea," downgrade your confidence until you have better evidence. Then place every open thread on the Interest Ladder, with evidence, and flag any where your last message assumed a higher rung than the evidence supports.

**Stop:** using warmth as a forecast. **Start:** checking the conditions a decision needs. **Notice:** when a new constraint changes readiness. **Measure:** fewer evaluations that reveal obvious fit or timing mismatches late.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Interest is not readiness. Silence is data, not rejection.</div>
