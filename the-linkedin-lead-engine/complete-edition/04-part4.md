# Chapter 13: CTAs That Create Conversations

<p class="lede">The CTA should match buyer intent, not your quota.</p>

Most LinkedIn CTAs are terrible: *"Agree?" "Thoughts?" "Comment YES."* They ask for effort without giving a reason, they produce noisy engagement that says nothing about demand, and LinkedIn has said it's working to reduce exactly this kind of engagement bait.

A call to action is a **continuation of the lesson**. The more effort it asks for, the more clearly the post must have earned it. Asking for a call at the end of an unrelated motivational story leaves a gap the reader has to bridge alone. Most won't.

## The CTA ladder

<div class="ladder">
<div><b>Commercial</b><span>"If you're solving this this quarter, message me about a pipeline review."</span></div>
<div><b>Conversation / DM</b><span>"If you're not sure whether it's qualification or ownership, message me the stage where it stalls."</span></div>
<div><b>Resource</b><span>"The one-page review sheet is linked below: use it on your next five proposals."</span></div>
<div><b>Experience</b><span>"When proposals stall in your team, which decision is usually unresolved?"</span></div>
<div><b>Application</b><span>"Check one stalled proposal for an owner, a decision and a date."</span></div>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 4. The CTA Ladder. Climb it only as far as the post has earned.</p>

- **Application** serves readers who aren't ready to engage publicly, which is most of them.
- **Experience** invites relevant detail, not agreement. "What's the biggest bottleneck you're seeing?" beats "Thoughts?"
- **Resource** says what the resource does and how to get it.
- **Conversation / DM** works only if you can actually respond usefully and quickly.
- **Commercial** makes it clear this is a paid service. That's honest, and it filters well.

**Pick the smallest action that moves the reader's actual task forward.** "Smaller" doesn't always mean "a freebie." A buyer with a clear need may prefer your pricing page.

## One primary action

A post that asks readers to comment, follow, repost, download, subscribe *and* book a call has given them six competing instructions and, usually, no reason for any of them. Choose one. Secondary routes can live on your profile.

## About keyword CTAs ("Comment 'FRAMEWORK' and I'll send it")

A keyword can be a convenient way to deliver a resource. Don't use one just to inflate your visible comment count. It produces noisy evidence about real demand, and it drifts toward the engagement bait LinkedIn says it's reducing. Where a direct link serves the reader better, use the link. **Never make praise, tagging friends or reposting the price of access.**

## Say what the next step costs

If a resource needs an email, say so. If a call is 20 minutes, say what it's for and what they'll leave with. If an audit is paid, say that before anyone invests time in a sales conversation. Clear expectations may cut raw responses and improve fit, which is the trade you want.

- **Weak:** "DM me to transform your business."
- **Better:** "Message me if you want the scope for a paid review of where your proposals stall. I'll first check whether your sales process is a fit for it."

## Action: three CTAs, one post

For your next post, write an application CTA, a resource CTA and a commercial CTA. Choose the one the body actually earns. If you want the commercial CTA but the post gives no reason to trust the offer, strengthen the **evidence**, not the urgency.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Ask for the smallest next step the post has earned, and make its cost clear.</div>

# Chapter 14: The Lead Magnet Post

<p class="lede">Don't build a lead magnet that attracts freebie hunters. Build one that advances a buying decision.</p>

Resource posts can generate extraordinary activity. They can also fill your inbox with people who will never buy anything. The difference is almost always the resource.

A useful lead magnet solves **a small, real problem next to your paid work**. It helps a suitable reader complete a task, diagnose a situation or make a decision. "The 500 Best AI Tools" will attract hundreds of people who don't need your service. "A one-page worksheet to find where your proposals stall" has an obvious relationship to sales-process consulting.

## Design backward from the paid service

1. Write down the **paid outcome** you deliver.
2. Identify the **decision** a buyer must make *before* they'd consider that service.
3. Design the resource around that decision.

| Paid outcome | Precursor decision | Good lead magnet | Weak lead magnet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clearer client onboarding (ops consultant) | Is the delay access, ownership, scope or scheduling? | A handoff checklist | A generic productivity ebook |
| Better interview performance for PM roles (career coach) | Where am I actually losing interviews? | A structured post-interview reflection sheet | Motivational quotes |
| Predictable outbound pipeline (B2B agency) | Is our problem volume, targeting or conversion? | A pipeline-leak diagnostic | "100 cold email templates" |
| Fractional CFO engagement | Do we understand our runway risk? | A 12-month cash-scenario template | "10 finance tips for founders" |

## The five-part resource post

<div class="flow">
<span><b>Problem</b></span><span><b>Insight</b><br>(delivered publicly)</span><span><b>Promise</b><br>what it helps you do</span><span><b>Contents + fit</b><br>who it's for, and not for</span><span><b>Access</b><br>clear, honest route</span>
</div>

<div class="post" markdown="1">
If every proposal needs founder approval, check the decision rules before you hire.

The bottleneck is usually "who can approve what," not capacity.

I built a one-page worksheet with five stages, three ownership states and a next-action field. It helps agency owners pick one handoff to fix first.

It doesn't calculate revenue loss or prescribe a full sales process.

The worksheet is at the link below. No email needed.
</div>

Notice the **boundary** ("it doesn't…"). A one-page resource shouldn't claim to diagnose an entire business. Overpromising can win downloads and lose trust the moment someone opens the file.

## Gated or ungated?

- **Ungated** when the goal is broad usefulness and low friction.
- **Email-gated** when ongoing delivery or a newsletter is genuinely part of the value. Say what they're signing up for, and keep resource access separate from optional promotions.
- **By DM request** only when conversation adds value *and* you can fulfill every request promptly.

**Deliver first.** "Here's the worksheet you asked for. Start with the stage where work waits longest. If you want to tell me which one you picked, I can help you use it." Never make a discovery call the price of a resource you promised.

## Measure the resource, not the downloads

Downloads show demand for *access*, not demand for your service. Track successful delivery, observable use, relevant questions, fit conversations and opportunities. After a sensible interval, you can ask one optional practical question ("Were you able to pick one handoff to review?"). Don't send repeated reminders to people who never asked for support.

## Action: the resource spec

Write a spec: **user, task, inputs, output, time required, limitation, delivery method, connection to your paid service.** Give a draft to two target users and watch where they hesitate. Fix the instructions before you promote it.

<div class="rule">Field rule: The best lead magnet is the first step of the work you get paid for.</div>

# Chapter 15: Turning Comments Into Conversations

<p class="lede">A viral post creates attention. Your comment section creates relationships.</p>

Your replies show how you treat people who aren't paying you. They also reveal whether your expertise holds up outside a prepared post. A thoughtful comment deserves a reply to its **substance**, not an automatic invitation to your calendar.

## Classify the comment first

| Comment contains… | Respond with… |
|---|---|
| Appreciation | A genuine thank-you. That's enough. |
| A specific experience | A follow-up question that narrows the situation |
| Disagreement | A clarification, a correction, or a concession if they're right |
| A resource request | Fulfillment, promptly |
| A buying question | A direct commercial answer |

- **Bad:** "Thanks! DM me if interested."
- **Better:** "Interesting that you mentioned hiring. Is the bigger issue getting enough candidates, or finding ones with the right experience?"

Now you're having a conversation.

## Respond with context

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Reader:** This keeps happening after procurement gets involved.

**Author:** Does the delay usually come from new requirements, or from waiting on approval of what was already agreed?
</div>

The question narrows things without claiming to know the cause. If they answer, contribute something useful before asking anything else. Don't turn the thread into a questionnaire.

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Reader:** What do you charge for this?

**Author:** It depends on scope. Happy to explain the standard review and what changes the price. Would you prefer the details here or by message?
</div>

If you have a published price, just share it. Don't hide a simple answer behind an unnecessary call.

## Buying signals, and what they don't prove

Signals worth noticing: the reader describes the problem *in their own business*, asks how something would work *for them*, asks about process, timing or price, or asks to talk. Each deserves a direct, relevant answer. None of them proves budget, authority or urgency on its own. Interest is not readiness.

## Moving to DM

Move to private messages when the person asks, when the context becomes sensitive, or when a specific exchange would be easier there. Ask first: "This might involve details you'd rather not post publicly. Want to continue by message?" **A public comment is not blanket permission for a sales sequence.**

Your first DM should carry the shared context: "You mentioned proposals stall after procurement joins. Happy to help think it through. Is it new requirements or approval timing?" If they already answered that publicly, don't ask again.

## A complete illustrative exchange

<div class="thread" markdown="1">
**Commenter:** We have an account manager, but everything still comes back to me.

**Author:** Which decisions come back most often: scope changes, pricing or client escalations?

**Commenter:** Mostly scope. Nobody wants to commit without checking.

**Author:** That's worth looking at as a decision rule. A job title doesn't say what someone can approve. Do you have written boundaries for small changes?

**Commenter:** Not written down. Can I message you?

**Author (DM):** Of course. Start with one recent request and what made it hard to approve. You can leave client details out.
</div>

Once context is shared, the commercial transition offers a real choice: *"The immediate step looks like defining which changes your account manager can approve. If you want help implementing that across the team, I can explain the paid review. If you just need a starting point, the worksheet covers the first decision."* It follows an expressed problem, and it doesn't pretend the commenter has already decided to buy.

## Follow-up boundaries

If you promised a resource, send it. If you agreed a date, follow up on that date. If a conversation stops without an agreement, one relevant check-in after a few business days is reasonable ("Checking whether you still wanted the scope for the review. If priorities moved, no need to continue"). **After a decline, stop.** Silence isn't a hidden objection you're entitled to overcome.

<div class="sidebar" markdown="1">
**Where this book hands off**

Everything after "Can I message you?" (reading the buyer's state, recognizing which trust question they're answering, choosing the smallest next move, knowing when *not* to send) is the subject of my companion book, *The Non-Salesy DM*. This book gets the right conversations started. That one helps you handle them without pressure.
</div>

## Action: five replies

Draft replies to five comment types: appreciation, disagreement, a problem description, a resource request and a price question. Each reply must answer what was actually said before it tries to move anything forward.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Reply to the substance, not the opportunity.</div>

# Chapter 16: The Profile-to-Post Conversion System

<p class="lede">The post makes a claim about your thinking. The profile has to confirm it.</p>

Someone reads your post. Then, often:

<div class="flow">
<span>Read post</span><span>Click profile</span><span>Read headline</span><span>Scan About</span><span>Check Featured</span><span>Decide whether to contact you</span>
</div>

People skip steps and browse in different orders, so treat this as a design model, not a law. But the requirement is universal: **coherence.** If your posts discuss agency sales processes and your profile lists six unrelated services, the reader has to do the work of figuring out your relevance. Most won't bother.

## Headline

Audience + problem or outcome + method or role.

*"Helping B2B agency owners reduce founder dependence in sales | Qualification, proposal and follow-up systems."*

Avoid stacking fashionable titles with no connecting idea. A reader shouldn't have to reconcile six identities to understand what you want to be known for.

## Banner

Reinforce a memorable problem or principle rather than repeating the headline. *"Where does your pipeline wait for you?"* Keep it legible, and check that your profile photo doesn't cover the important part on mobile.

## About section

A practical structure:

1. **"You may be here because…"** followed by two recognizable situations.
2. **"The work focuses on…"** followed by scope.
3. **"My approach…"** followed by process.
4. **"Evidence…"** followed by verified proof or an honest sample.
5. **"Next step…"** followed by one clear route.

Plain paragraphs. Specific achievements only when you can support them.

## Featured and the contact path

Pick a small set of assets with distinct jobs: **an entry resource, evidence of your method, service details.** Label them by reader value. "Proposal handoff worksheet" beats "My latest PDF."

Then check every destination on your phone. A broken link, an unreadable page, a vague contact form or a resource that never arrives can waste all the attention your writing earned. **The conversion path includes what happens after the click.**

## The stranger test

Show your profile to someone who hasn't heard your pitch. Give them 20 seconds, then ask:

1. Who is this for?
2. What problem does this person solve?
3. What makes that believable?
4. What would you do next?

Their answers are worth far more than "does it look professional?"

## Action: the coherence trace

Put one recent post, your headline, your About section and your first destination link side by side. Underline the shared buyer problem. If it changes at every step, pick one coherent path and fix it before you drive more traffic.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Every click after the post should say the same thing the post said.</div>

# Chapter 17: From Content to Client

<p class="lede">Content reduces uncertainty before a conversation. It can't replace the conversation.</p>

A lead engine needs a commercial process that can handle the interest it creates. Four checks tell you whether a conversation is worth evaluating further: **problem, fit, decision path and next step.** You need enough information to decide, not a fully completed qualification form.

## Qualify through relevant questions

- **Problem:** "What's happening now, and what does it interrupt?"
- **Fit:** "Which part would you want help changing?"
- **Decision path:** "Who else would need to evaluate a project like this?"
- **Timing:** "Is this something you want to address soon, or are you collecting options?"

Ask about resources *in context*, and share your commercial frame first: "The standard engagement is in this fee range and needs these people involved. Is that realistic for what you have in mind?" Never withhold your price while asking someone to disclose their budget.

Someone without budget right now can still be a valuable relationship. They just aren't an opportunity *yet*. Record the difference, and never treat them dismissively.

## Discovery calls with an outcome

Before inviting a call, say what it will resolve: "A 20-minute conversation would let us map where proposals wait, check whether it's in scope, and decide whether a review makes sense." Offer a written alternative when the questions can be answered that way.

During the call, cover the current state, the desired change, constraints, previous attempts and the decision process. Reflect back your understanding. Give your recommendation with its boundaries. End with an agreed action, an owner and a date, or a clear decision not to proceed.

## Objections are information

| They say | A useful response |
|---|---|
| "We can do this internally." | "That may be the right choice. The worksheet gives you a start. Outside help is most useful when capacity, cross-team decisions or implementation are the blocker. Do any of those apply?" |
| "It's too expensive." | "Is it the available budget, the scope, or uncertainty about the value? If budget's the constraint, let's be direct about it." |
| "Send me information." | "Sure. I'll send scope, requirements, fee and a sample output. Is there one question you need it to answer?" |
| "Not now." | "Understood. Is there a point when a reminder would help, or would you rather come back to it yourself?" Then honor the answer. |

Reduce scope only when the smaller engagement still works. Never promise the same work at an arbitrary discount.

## Close with a clear agreement

A proposal connects the buyer's situation to deliverables, responsibilities, schedule, fee, payment terms, exclusions and acceptance steps. Don't turn a hoped-for business result into a guarantee you can't control. Specify the work you'll deliver and the outcomes you'll measure together. A verbal yes isn't a completed sale: follow the agreed contracting and payment process, then deliver the first promised step.

## An end-to-end example (illustrative)

1. A consultant who helps small B2B agencies fix sales handoffs posts about why proposals stall after a positive call. **An agency founder saves it but doesn't comment.**
2. A later post compares two causes: unclear commercial fit vs. unclear decision ownership. **The founder realizes several deals are waiting on internal approval, not another price revision.**
3. A third post offers a worksheet for reviewing five recent opportunities. **The founder requests it.** The consultant records a *resource request*, not an opportunity, and delivers it with an offer to help them use it.
4. The founder replies: *"Three of the five have no agreed review date. We keep sending reminders."* **Now there's a substantive conversation about a real problem.** The consultant suggests a small fix the team can try on its own.
5. The founder asks whether the consultant can make that consistent across the team. The consultant shares the paid review's scope, fee, participation and exclusions. The founder confirms it's a priority and brings in the operations lead. **Now it's a qualified opportunity.**
6. On the call, the consultant learns the team also needs a complex CRM migration, which is outside scope. They separate it rather than quietly absorbing it. The narrower scope is accepted, and the proposal records deliverables, responsibilities, timing and terms.
7. Afterward, the consultant asks what prompted the inquiry. The founder mentions all three posts **and** a peer's recommendation. The record notes content influence and the referral. It doesn't claim the resource post alone produced the sale.

This is one progression, not a required sequence. Some buyers arrive asking about price on day one. Others use the worksheet and never need paid help. The engine works when every step serves the actual situation.

## Action: the opportunity record

Create a one-page record: source post or referral, buyer situation, fit evidence, open questions, stakeholders, timing, next step, agreed follow-up date and status. If you can't fill in the fit evidence and the next step, it's still a conversation. Don't inflate your pipeline.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Content starts the decision. A clear, honest process finishes it.</div>
