# Chapter 18: Build Your Content Pillars

<p class="lede">A pillar is a durable area of buyer concern, not a label broad enough to hold the internet.</p>

"Business," "mindset" and "marketing" don't help you choose tomorrow's post. Pillars exist so that repetition builds **recognition** instead of monotony.

## Five sources of material

1. **What you know:** frameworks, checklists, methods.
2. **What you've experienced:** real projects, decisions, mistakes.
3. **What you believe:** reasoned positions, including unpopular ones.
4. **What you've learned:** how your thinking has changed, and why.
5. **What your customers need:** their questions, objections, language.

These are *inputs*. Translate them into **buyer-facing themes** before anything goes on the calendar.

## From your knowledge to their decisions

For a consultant focused on agency sales, pillars might be:

1. Diagnosing pipeline problems
2. Clarifying the offer
3. Improving sales-to-delivery handoffs
4. Reducing founder dependence
5. Evaluating implementation choices

Each pillar draws on all five sources. "What I know" produces a qualification checklist. "What I experienced" supplies a real handoff story. "What I believe" supports an argument against premature automation. "What I learned" explains a revised process. "What customers need" reveals the next objection to address.

**Don't create a pillar just because you enjoy the topic.** Keep a little room for broader interests if they support your professional identity. But the more unrelated territory you cover, the harder it is for a new reader to remember what you do.

## The problem bank

Keep a simple bank with five fields: **buyer phrase, situation, decision, available evidence, possible next step.** Capture entries right after calls and delivery work, while the context is fresh.

<div class="example" markdown="1">
**Example record.** *Buyer phrase:* "We get inquiries, but most want something cheaper." *Situation:* a specialist agency attracting early-stage businesses. *Decision:* change targeting, offer or qualifying information? *Evidence:* interview notes and inquiry categories. *Next step:* check the promise in the lead magnet and profile.
</div>

That one record can become an **attraction** post about the symptom, an **authority** post comparing causes, a **relationship** question about trade-offs and a **conversion** post describing a focused review. Same problem, four different jobs.

## Repurpose by changing the decision

Repurposing should add a new use, not just reformat the same sentences. Turn a case into a diagnostic, the diagnostic into a demonstration, the demonstration into an objection answer, and the objection into an implementation guide. When you revisit a topic, bring a new example, boundary, question or piece of evidence.

## A weekly research habit

Spend one short session a week reviewing buyer language, completed work, unanswered comments and changes in your field. Select ideas by audience relevance and evidence strength. A good idea bank contains plenty of material you're choosing *not* to publish yet.

## Action: five pillars

Create five buyer-facing pillars. Under each, write three decisions a customer faces and two evidence sources you can access. Remove any pillar that can't produce both a useful post and a plausible connection to your work. (The Content Engine Tracker's Problem Bank tab has these fields.)

<div class="rule">Field rule: Pillars are the problems you want to be remembered for solving.</div>

# Chapter 19: The 30-Day Lead Plan

<p class="lede">The first month builds a working process and an evidence base. It's too short to promise revenue.</p>

Treat your first 30 days as the first learning cycle of an ongoing system. B2B buying cycles are often longer than a month. So judge it by whether the engine works, and let the commercial results mature.

## Choose your cadence

| | **Standard** (3 posts/week) | **Intensive** (5 posts/week) |
|---|---|---|
| Posts in 30 days | 12 | 20 |
| Best for | Consultants with delivery work; first-time systematic posters | Operators with time and material to spare |
| Weekly mix | 1 attraction · 1 authority · 1 relationship *or* conversion | 1 attraction · 2 authority · 1 relationship · 1 conversion |

**Intensive weekly rhythm:** Monday, story (attraction) · Tuesday, how-to (authority) · Wednesday, contrarian (attraction/authority) · Thursday, case study or demonstration (authority) · Friday, resource or offer (conversion). Swap one slot for a conversation post (relationship) when you need it.

If you publish fewer posts, extend the sequence rather than squeezing out the research and evidence checks. A slower system you can maintain beats an ambitious calendar you abandon in week two.

## The Standard four-week sequence

| Week | Purpose | Posts | Supporting work |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Establish relevance | Day 5 symptom · Day 7 method · Day 9 field question | Audience, profile, baseline, proof ledger |
| **2** | Demonstrate judgment | Day 12 comparison · Day 14 case/demonstration · Day 16 resource | Build and test the resource; handle replies |
| **3** | Connect interest to fit | Day 19 objection answer · Day 21 lesson learned · Day 23 offer | Review conversations and the commercial path |
| **4** | Improve from evidence | Day 25 sharper field question · Day 27 method variation · Day 29 commercial FAQ | Diagnose results; design the next cycle |

Chapter 26 turns this into daily assignments.

## Run one experiment

Start with one question, such as: *"Do posts about proposal ownership attract more fit conversations than broad sales advice?"* Keep the audience and offer consistent, vary the example and engine, and track both reach and the **nature** of responses.

Don't change audience, positioning, frequency, format, offer and CTA all at once and then claim you found "the best hook." You won't know what worked.

## Plan your response capacity

Before a resource post, estimate how many requests you can fulfill and how fast. Prepare the resource, the access instructions and the delivery message, and test the link on a phone. Before an offer post, make sure you can deliver the scope and onboard a client. If capacity is limited, say so honestly. Never invent a scarcity story.

## A sustainable time budget

A starting week: **45 minutes of research, two 60-minute drafting and editing sessions, three short reply sessions, 30 minutes of review.** Track your actual time for two weeks before deciding whether content is "worth it." If you only have 90 minutes a week, publish one strong post, reply carefully and review a few signals.

## Action: put it on the calendar

Put your publication dates and four review sessions on your calendar now. Give each post a buyer problem, a primary job, an evidence source and a CTA. If any field is unknown, resolving it becomes a task before publication.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Consistency means reliably doing useful work, not keeping a streak alive.</div>

# Chapter 20: The Post Creation Workflow

<p class="lede">Separate finding, drafting, editing, publishing and learning.</p>

Mixing these creates friction: you research while polishing a sentence, invent a hook before you know the point, and check analytics while trying to finish the body. Here's the workflow in fifteen steps.

<div class="table-caption">The 15-Step Workflow</div>

| # | Step | What "done" looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Find** | | |
| 1 | **Find the problem** | A specific buyer situation from your problem bank: who has it, and when |
| 2 | **Find the insight** | The explanation or decision rule in one sentence. "This is important" doesn't count. |
| 3 | **Find the story or demonstration** | Evidence that supports the insight. If there's no true story, use a labeled hypothetical or a process. |
| 4 | **Write ten hooks** | Varied angles, each a promise the evidence can keep |
| **Build** | | |
| 5 | **Choose the strongest hook** | Clear relevance and a deliverable promise beat theatrical language |
| 6 | **Write the rehook** | Adds context, tension or a preview; no repeated meaning |
| 7 | **Build the body** | Pick an engine; each paragraph answers the question the last one raised |
| 8 | **Add proof** | Example, artifact, source or measurement details, with permission and limits checked |
| 9 | **Add the payoff** | What the reader now understands or can do; it resolves the hook |
| 10 | **Write the CTA** | Matches awareness and the post's job. No CTA beats an irrelevant one. |
| **Ship and learn** | | |
| 11 | **Remove fluff** | Unsupported superlatives, repeated claims and self-praise gone |
| 12 | **Format for mobile** | Preview it; the opening makes sense on its own; links clearly labeled |
| 13 | **Publish** | Right account, audience, attachments and destination; timed for your readers *and* your availability to reply |
| 14 | **Engage** | Answer relevant questions; correct misunderstandings; never fake activity |
| 15 | **Analyze** | Consistent observation window; metrics *and* conversation quality; the next question this post revealed |

## The ten-line post brief

Before writing, fill in: **reader · situation · awareness rung · primary job · thesis · engine · evidence · boundary · payoff · CTA.** Ten lines can save an hour of unfocused editing.

<div class="example" markdown="1">
**Example brief.** *Reader:* agency founder. *Situation:* proposals need founder approval. *Awareness:* problem aware. *Job:* authority. *Thesis:* unclear decision rights may be creating the queue. *Engine:* how-to. *Evidence:* sample decision matrix. *Boundary:* doesn't fix poor offer fit. *Payoff:* define approval rules for one recurring request type. *CTA:* apply the check to a recent proposal.
</div>

## The quality gate (V.A.L.U.E.)

Before publishing, mark each as **ready / revise / unknown**:

- **Visibility:** Can the intended reader recognize the subject in the opening?
- **Attention:** Does the rehook add meaning, and does the body keep the promise?
- **Legitimacy:** Are claims supported, examples labeled, limits proportionate?
- **Usefulness:** Can the reader make a better decision or complete a task?
- **Engagement-to-Action:** Is the next step clear, appropriate and working?

Don't add the marks up into a pretend virality score. Fix every "unknown" fact and every broken destination before you publish. A beautiful phrase doesn't make up for a missing explanation.

## Action: time one post

Run one idea through all fifteen steps without skipping the proof check. Record the time each phase takes. If drafting is slow because evidence is missing, improve how you capture it. If editing is slow because the thesis keeps changing, improve the brief.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Don't write a hook until you know the point.</div>

# Chapter 21: Measuring What Actually Matters

<p class="lede">Measure the business without inventing attribution.</p>

Measurement exists to help you decide what to do next. A dashboard full of activity counts can distract from that job. Start with a small set of clearly defined measures, and keep the definitions stable long enough to compare them.

LinkedIn's own analytics define **impressions** as the number of times a post was shown and **members reached** as the distinct members who saw it. LinkedIn describes these as estimates, and some counts can include repeat or your own activity. Check the current definitions in your analytics before comparing numbers.

## Three layers

| Layer | Measures | What it tells you | What it doesn't |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Distribution** | Impressions, members reached, audience data | Exposure | Whether viewers fit |
| **Response** | Relevant comments, substantive DMs, resource use, profile and link activity | Interest and friction | Buyer qualification |
| **Commercial progression** | Fit conversations, qualified opportunities, proposals, won work, cash collected | Business results | Which single post "caused" it |

A like is a lighter signal than a detailed description of a problem, and neither automatically qualifies anyone.

## Define the denominator

- **Fit conversation rate** = fit conversations ÷ substantive new conversations. (Six of twelve = 50%. It is *not* six divided by every reaction and impression.)
- **Opportunity rate** = qualified opportunities ÷ fit conversations.
- **Proposal win rate** = won ÷ *decided* proposals. Report open proposals separately.
- **Landing-page conversion** = completed resource requests ÷ measured page sessions. Don't silently swap in LinkedIn link clicks.

Always state the time period and cohort, especially when decisions happen weeks after the post.

<div class="table-caption">An Illustrative Monthly Record (invented for instruction; not a benchmark)</div>

| Measure | Count | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Posts published | 12 | Output, not success |
| Substantive new conversations | 12 | People discussing a real issue |
| Fit conversations | 6 | Relevant audience and problem |
| Qualified opportunities | 3 | Fit + decision path + agreed next step |
| Proposals sent | 2 | A commercial proposal made sense |
| Won | 1 | Met your documented win definition |
| Open | 1 | Still undecided. Not a loss. |

## Track contribution, not sole cause

Ask every new prospect how they found you and what made them reach out. Record the first known touch, any content they mention, the immediate trigger and other channels involved. Real paths look like this: a post, a referral, several articles, then a reply to an email. Report **sourced** and **influenced** opportunities separately, under definitions you write down, and never credit the full deal to every channel that touched it.

Revenue isn't profit. Compare cash collected with delivery costs and with the time content and conversations take. Pipeline value isn't cash either, and an arbitrary close probability doesn't make it cash.

## Diagnose the pattern

| Pattern | Likely area to inspect |
|---|---|
| Low reach, but relevant responses | Distribution or audience size |
| High reach, poor fit | Topics too broad; positioning drift |
| Relevant profile visits, little contact | Offer clarity or next step |
| Many resource requests, little use | The promise, the audience or the resource itself |
| Calls with poor fit | Qualification or targeting |

These are hypotheses. Read actual examples before changing the system. One bad week can be normal variation, timing or a slow buying cycle.

## Review rhythm

Take an early snapshot for operational issues, and a consistent later one (say, seven days after publishing) for comparison. Review commercial progression monthly and by cohort over your buying cycle. Keep older posts attached to opportunities they later influence, rather than declaring their value over after a week.

## Action: set up the record

Track post ID, audience, job, evidence, responses, opportunities and time spent. (The Content Engine Tracker's Metrics tab calculates these rates for you.) Choose your next experiment from the biggest *documented* bottleneck.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Measure qualified attention. Everything else is a leading indicator at best.</div>
