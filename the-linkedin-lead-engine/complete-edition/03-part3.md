# Chapter 9: The Right-Person Principle

<p class="lede">A great post doesn't appeal to everyone. It makes your ideal buyer feel unusually understood.</p>

Instead of: *"5 ways to improve your marketing."*
Write: *"5 things SaaS founders should stop doing if their pipeline depends on founder-led sales."*

The second one has a smaller audience. It also has a far better one, and that's exactly what we want.

## Broad → Specific → Commercially relevant

| Broad | Specific | Commercially relevant |
|---|---|---|
| "Better marketing" | "More consistent inquiries for B2B consultants" | "Why inquiries from your lead magnet don't match the service you sell" |
| "Leadership lessons" | "Delegation for agency founders" | "Which approvals your account leads should own before you hire another salesperson" |
| "Financial tips for startups" | "Cash planning for seed-stage SaaS" | "The three numbers your board will ask about 9 months after your seed round" |
| "Career advice" | "Interview prep for product managers" | "How senior PM candidates lose the offer in the case-study round" |

The final column sits right next to a decision the reader has to make, which is where buying begins.

Commercial relevance doesn't mean every post mentions your offer. It means the **problem territory** connects to work you can credibly help with. A thoughtful post on an adjacent topic can build relationships, but a feed dominated by unrelated topics makes your commercial identity hard to remember.

## Start with one audience

An audience definition is only useful when it changes what you write. "Entrepreneurs who want growth" is too broad to decide anything. "Owners of B2B service agencies whose proposals still need founder approval" points you toward recognizable situations, relevant examples and a plausible service.

Pick **one primary audience for your first 30-day cycle.** It's a publishing focus, not a permanent ban on other clients. Changing the buyer, the problem and the offer every week makes your results impossible to read.

### The buyer situation statement

> "I help **[role and business context]** dealing with **[observable problem]** when **[trigger or constraint]**, so they can **[valuable change]**."

Then add who is **outside scope**.

*Example:* "I help owners of small B2B agencies whose sales still depend on their daily involvement build clearer qualification, proposal and follow-up routines." *Outside scope:* businesses without a validated offer; companies wanting outsourced high-volume prospecting.

Company size, geography and industry matter only when they change the problem, the purchasing process or your ability to deliver. Don't add filters just because a template has empty boxes.

## Build a language bank

Collect the words buyers actually use in calls, emails, support threads, interviews and public discussions. Record the exact phrase, the context, the underlying task and whether you're allowed to publish it. "Every proposal comes back to me" is far more useful than "founder bottleneck optimization."

Ask five people in your audience:

1. What happened the last time this problem came up?
2. What did you try?
3. What made the current approach acceptable until now?
4. What would improvement change?
5. What trade-offs would make a solution unattractive?

Five interviews aren't market research. They're a source of better questions and hypotheses. If every answer describes a different problem, narrow the context or rethink the offer.

## The relevance filter

Before drafting any post, complete three lines:

1. **"This is for…"**
2. **"They recognize the problem when…"**
3. **"After reading, they can…"**

If line three is just "feel inspired," ask whether that's really the post's purpose. Inspiration has value, but it shouldn't quietly substitute for a practical promise.

## Action: ten topics, three keepers

Turn ten broad topics into buyer situations using the three-column table. Keep the three you can explain with evidence. Show one to a target reader without your audience statement: can they tell who it's for? If not, it's still too generic.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Smaller audience, better audience. Write for the person who would pay.</div>

# Chapter 10: The Authority Equation

<p class="lede">Don't tell people you're an expert. Show them how you think.</p>

Authority is a conclusion the *reader* reaches about your ability to help. Declaring yourself an expert does almost nothing to create it. Showing how you investigate a problem, choose between options and explain the limits gives the reader something to evaluate.

<div class="equation">
<div class="eq-main"><span>Experience</span> + <span>Evidence</span> + <span>Insight</span> = Authority</div>
<div class="eq-parts">
<div><b>Experience</b><br>Real contact with the work</div>
<div><b>Evidence</b><br>Something the reader can inspect</div>
<div><b>Insight</b><br>Why the evidence matters</div>
</div>
</div>

Instead of *"I'm an expert in sales,"* show something like *"We tested four outbound approaches across 137 opportunities. Here's what happened."* (That's the shape of the claim, not a real statistic. Use your own numbers.)

## Authority tools, ranked by what they prove

| Tool | Proves… | Watch out for… |
|---|---|---|
| **Documented result** | You've produced outcomes like this | Comparability to the reader's situation; permission |
| **Process demonstration** | How you work | Making it concrete enough to evaluate |
| **Artifact** (a redacted audit, decision matrix or checklist) | Your method is real | Client confidentiality |
| **Experiment** | You test rather than guess | Small samples; confounders |
| **Mistake / lesson learned** | Judgment and honesty | Turning it into disguised bragging |
| **Strong opinion with a mechanism** | You understand cause and effect | Opinions without a "because" |
| **Behind-the-scenes observation** | Proximity to the work | Oversharing |
| **Framework** | You can simplify complexity | A diagram doesn't validate itself |

**New practitioners:** don't manufacture results to look experienced. Analyze a public example (with clear limits), build a demonstration with sample data, document a small project you actually completed, or publish a reasoned comparison. Say what the evidence shows and what it doesn't. That honesty *is* an authority signal.

## The evidence capsule

For any results post, include six things: **context, baseline, action, time period, how you defined the outcome, and a limitation.**

- **Weak:** "We increased conversions by 40%."
- **Stronger (if true):** "In a four-week test, completed inquiry forms rose from 10 of 200 visits to 14 of 200. Small sample, and the traffic mix may have changed, so we can't credit the rewrite for all of it."

That's a 40% *relative* increase and a two-*percentage-point* increase, and knowing the difference is itself a credibility signal. (Hypothetical numbers, used only to show the structure.)

## Strong opinions need a mechanism

"More tools won't fix your operations" is familiar and forgettable. "If two teams define a 'qualified lead' differently, a shared dashboard just displays their disagreement faster" explains *how* the problem happens. Follow the mechanism with a decision rule ("agree the definition and owner before choosing dashboard fields"), and the reader can apply your thinking and judge it.

## Handle thin evidence honestly

Use "in this example," "my working hypothesis" or "the client reported" when there's a real limit, not as decoration on an exaggerated claim. If the uncertainty undermines your main promise, change the promise.

Remember what each kind of evidence proves. A framework diagram doesn't validate a framework. A screenshot doesn't establish cause. A testimonial doesn't prove typical results.

## Action: the proof ledger

Create a ledger with columns for **claim, source, date, permission, limitations and approved wording.** Add five claims you could publish today. Delete any for which you can't find the underlying record. (It's a tab in the Content Engine Tracker.)

<div class="rule">Field rule: Authority is shown, not stated. Every claim needs a receipt.</div>

# Chapter 11: The Problem-Awareness Ladder

<p class="lede">Two people can have the same problem and need completely different posts.</p>

One reader hasn't noticed the problem yet. Another has compared providers and wants to know whether your implementation fits their team. Giving both the same educational introduction wastes everyone's attention.

<div class="ladder">
<div><b>Ready to buy</b><span>"Who the implementation sprint is for, and what it requires"</span></div>
<div><b>Provider aware</b><span>"What a sales-handoff audit actually includes"</span></div>
<div><b>Solution aware</b><span>"Decision rules vs. another sales hire"</span></div>
<div><b>Problem aware</b><span>"3 reasons your pipeline dries up every quarter"</span></div>
<div><b>Unaware</b><span>"Your sales team might not have a lead problem"</span></div>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 3. The Problem-Awareness Ladder. A planning aid: people skip rungs, go backward, and sit on different rungs for different parts of a purchase.</p>

| Reader state | The post's job | Example | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | Make a symptom recognizable | "Why a busy founder can still be the slowest stage in sales" | Check one workflow |
| **Problem aware** | Explain causes and consequences | "Three reasons proposals wait for founder input" | Use a diagnostic |
| **Solution aware** | Compare approaches | "How we built predictable outbound without hiring 10 SDRs" | Compare options |
| **Provider aware** | Show fit and evidence | "What changed after we rebuilt a client's outbound system" | Review a sample |
| **Ready to buy** | Clarify scope and decision | "We're taking on [real number] implementation projects this quarter for B2B teams with founder-dependent sales" | Discuss the project |

*(Only state capacity limits that are real. Fake scarcity is a trust leak.)*

## One problem, climbing the ladder

Suppose you help service businesses reduce onboarding delays:

- **Unaware:** a story showing how small access delays pile up.
- **Problem aware:** a way to tell client-caused delay from internal handoff failure.
- **Solution aware:** automation vs. process redesign vs. account ownership: when each fits.
- **Provider aware:** your deliverables, your boundaries, a sample output.
- **Ready to buy:** timing, pricing logic, responsibilities, decision process.

**Education doesn't always have to come before the offer.** Some readers discover your profile when they're already shopping for a provider. Make your commercial information *findable*, even when most of your feed teaches.

## The awareness mismatch

A cold reader who sees "Book your strategy call" doesn't know what problem the call solves. A ready buyer who reads another inspirational post still can't find your scope. Both are **matching** failures, not writing failures.

The fix isn't a rigid sequence everyone must follow. It's a portfolio with clear routes: a diagnostic post that points to a resource, a case study that points to service details, a profile with an obvious entry point for readers arriving at any stage.

## Read signals without overreading them

- "This happens every month" → problem recognition.
- "Have you used this with a remote team?" → evaluation.
- "What would you need from us?" → possible implementation interest.

Each deserves a relevant answer. **None of them proves budget, authority or urgency on its own.** Answer the question first, then ask one proportionate follow-up. Don't interrogate someone because your funnel diagram says they've "entered a stage." (For how to read these conversations in depth, see *The Non-Salesy DM*.)

## Action: five rungs, one problem

Write five premises for one problem, one per rung, each with a different next step. Check that your ready-to-buy post contains real offer information, and that your unaware post makes sense to someone who's never heard of your framework.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Match the post to where the reader is, not to where you want them to be.</div>

# Chapter 12: The Four Posts Every Business Needs

<p class="lede">Attract → Educate → Prove → Convert → Nurture.</p>

A healthy content strategy isn't "write five viral posts." It's a portfolio of four jobs:

<div class="moves">
<div><b>Attraction</b><span>Reach the right new people</span></div>
<div><b>Authority</b><span>Prove your thinking</span></div>
<div><b>Relationship</b><span>Deepen connection and community</span></div>
<div><b>Conversion</b><span>Make the next step clear</span></div>
</div>

| Job | Goal | Best engines | Success looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attraction** | New relevant eyeballs | Story, contrarian, list, trend | Relevant new followers, profile visits from your audience |
| **Authority** | Trust | How-to, case study, lessons learned, data | Saves, specific questions, "how would this work for us?" |
| **Relationship** | Community and retention | Conversation, behind the scenes, milestones | Substantive replies, returning commenters |
| **Conversion** | Buying intent | Offer with boundaries, commercial FAQ, objection answer | Fit conversations, qualified opportunities |

A feed of only attraction posts becomes entertaining but commercially vague. A feed of only offers gives strangers no reason to believe you.

## A starting mix

For a five-post week: **1 attraction · 2 authority · 1 relationship · 1 conversion.** For three posts a week, rotate attraction and relationship while keeping one useful explanation and an occasional clear offer visible.

That's a starting allocation, not a proven ratio. And don't publish five times just to satisfy a calendar. If three good posts leave you time to research, reply and deliver, three is better. **Consistency means reliably doing useful work, not keeping a streak alive at any cost.**

## Topic, engine and job are different things

- **Topic** is *what* you discuss: proposal delays.
- **Engine** is *how* you organize it: a story.
- **Job** is *why* you publish it: authority.

Mixing them up makes your calendar repetitive. You can write several posts on proposal delays with different engines, for different awareness rungs, doing different jobs, without ever repeating the same lesson.

A usable planning record has: audience, problem, awareness rung, job, engine, evidence, CTA and review date. Keep it simple. A content database that takes longer to maintain than the posts is a burden, not a system. (The Content Engine Tracker has exactly these columns and nothing more.)

## Sell without apologizing, and without repeating yourself

A direct offer is appropriate whenever you can describe the fit and deliver on the promise. You don't need to apologize for selling. Explain the problem, scope, outcome, requirements and next step. Never fabricate scarcity, and never treat your service as a secret revealed only in DMs.

But if *every* post ends with the same pitch, even your best educational material starts to feel like advertising. Vary the next step to match the reader's task. Some posts should end with a useful instruction and no request at all.

## Action: label your last ten

Label your last ten posts (or ten draft premises) by primary job. Find the missing job and write one post to fill it. Don't rebalance just to hit a percentage. Say which reader need the new post serves.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Attract the right people, prove you can help, stay in touch, and make buying easy. Every week.</div>
