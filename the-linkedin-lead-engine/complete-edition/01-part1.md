# Chapter 1: Stop Chasing Viral

<p class="lede">Don't maximize attention. Maximize valuable attention.</p>

Two posts from the same consultant, one week apart.

**Post A** is a story about perseverance: the year everything went wrong and what it taught her. Thousands of people recognize themselves in it. 80,000 impressions. 1,900 reactions. 300 comments, most of them some version of "So needed this today."

**Post B** explains why agency proposals stall after a verbal "yes": usually because nobody owns the internal decision. 4,000 impressions. 60 reactions. 11 comments. Two of those comments are agency founders describing that exact problem in their own business. One sends a DM that becomes a paid project.

*(Illustrative example.)*

By every number on the dashboard, Post A won. By the only number that pays for anything, Post B won. And the consultant's natural reaction, "write more posts like A," is exactly the wrong lesson.

## Viral ≠ valuable

Most people believe LinkedIn content has one job: get engagement. That belief quietly shapes everything: the topics you pick, the hooks you write, the posts you repeat.

But a post can be widely shared because it's funny, moving, controversial or culturally familiar. None of those qualities tells you whether its readers have a problem you solve. **Reach creates possibilities. Audience, meaning and the next step decide which possibilities are useful.**

<div class="table-caption">Vanity LinkedIn vs. Business LinkedIn</div>

| Vanity LinkedIn optimizes for… | Business LinkedIn optimizes for… |
|---|---|
| More impressions | More *relevant* impressions |
| More likes | More authority with the right people |
| More followers | More profile visits from potential buyers |
| More comments | More qualified conversations |
| A viral post | Opportunities, and eventually revenue |

A viral post is a crowd that gathers for ten seconds. The engine is what decides whether any of them walk somewhere afterward.

## The two equations

It helps to think of it this way:

<div class="equation">
<div class="eq-main">Viral content ≈ <span>Attention</span> × Relevance × Value × Emotion × Distribution</div>
<div class="eq-main" style="margin-top:6px">Qualified leads ≈ Reach × <span>Audience fit</span> × <span>Authority</span> × Problem awareness × Trust × Conversion path</div>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 1. The two equations. They're diagnostic reminders, not arithmetic. The terms don't share units.</p>

The point isn't to calculate anything. It's that the second equation contains **every weak link that more reach can't fix**:

- The wrong people respond? Fix **audience fit** and topic.
- The right people read but can't explain what you do? Fix **authority** and positioning.
- Readers ask good questions but never progress? Fix the **conversion path**: your offer, your next step, your replies.

More impressions can't repair any of those. That's why doubling your reach so often doubles the noise, not the pipeline.

## Qualified attention

This book's central unit is **qualified attention**: attention from people whose situation makes your expertise relevant.

Some of them will buy. Some will influence a purchase, introduce you to someone, or remember you months later. Others will learn something useful and leave. A good engine serves all of them honestly, while making the commercial path obvious to the right ones.

The goal isn't to get 100,000 people to like your post. It's to get the right 100 to 1,000 people to think: **"This person understands my problem. I should talk to them."**

## Define useful success before you write

Give every post one **primary job**:

- **Attraction:** introduce a relevant idea to new, relevant people.
- **Authority:** make your thinking more credible.
- **Relationship:** deepen understanding or invite worthwhile discussion.
- **Conversion:** help an appropriate reader take a defined next step.

Then judge the post by *its* job. Don't change the definition of success after you publish. If a conversion post earns likes but no interest in the offer, the likes don't retroactively become the goal. And if an authority post helps one real prospect resolve an objection, low reach doesn't make it a failure. (Chapter 12 turns these four jobs into a posting mix.)

A **qualified opportunity** is much more demanding than an interaction. In this book it means an identifiable person or organization with a relevant problem, a plausible fit with your offer, a credible path to a decision, and an agreed next step. A download is not an opportunity. A complimentary comment is not an opportunity.

## The transformation

You start as someone who asks *"What should I post?"* By the end of this book you'll know:

1. How to create **attention**
2. How to create **authority**
3. How to attract your **ideal buyer** specifically
4. How to run a **repeatable content system** that produces conversations and leads

It's less a writing book than a LinkedIn **demand-generation** playbook that happens to be about writing.

## Action: your content objective

Write one sentence:

> "Over the next 30 days, I want **[specific audience]** to recognize **[specific problem]** and consider **[appropriate next step]**."

Then list three signals of progress you can actually observe (relevant replies, resource use, substantive DMs, fit conversations) and one commercial outcome you'll track over a longer period.

- ☐ One primary audience is named.
- ☐ The problem is specific enough to recognize in a real conversation.
- ☐ The next step is useful even if the reader never buys.
- ☐ Success includes audience *quality*, not reach alone.

<div class="rule">Field rule: A post that reaches 4,000 of the right people beats one that reaches 80,000 of the wrong ones.</div>

# Chapter 2: What Viral Posts Can (and Can't) Teach You

<p class="lede">Study the decisions behind a post, not its surface.</p>

Studying successful posts is useful when you study *decisions*: how the writer introduced a problem, set the stakes, supplied evidence and rewarded the reader. It's much less useful when you copy surface patterns and assume they caused the result.

## The survivorship trap

A dataset made up only of viral posts can tell you what appears among viral posts. Without a comparison group, it can't tell you whether the same features appear in thousands of ignored posts too.

If most winning posts use "I" and "you," that doesn't prove adding "I" creates reach. The author's audience, topic, timing, reputation and existing distribution all explain part of the difference. Personal, conversational writing is very often good *writing*. Just don't mistake a correlation in winners for a lever you can pull.

### The research quality filter

Before you adopt any "LinkedIn study says…" claim, ask:

- Who collected the data, and when?
- How were posts selected? What counted as "viral"?
- Was paid promotion excluded?
- Did it compare similar authors and topics?
- Was the outcome reach, engagement, leads or revenue? (These are not interchangeable.)

The large viral-post study behind this book's original concept couldn't pass this check, because its dataset and method weren't available to me. You don't need to wait for perfect research to write better posts. You *do* need to stop treating unverified figures as proof, including the ones in viral threads about LinkedIn itself.

## What LinkedIn has actually said

LinkedIn has publicly described feed changes intended to favor relevant professional content and reduce generic, recycled posts and **engagement bait** (empty "agree?" or "comment YES" prompts). It has also said it acts against **engagement pods** and **automated comments** as inauthentic activity.

That supports a sensible emphasis on relevance and substance. It does *not* reveal a dependable trick for making any particular post travel. And none of it establishes a universal best posting time, a mandatory "first-hour" reply window, an ideal line count, or a guaranteed penalty for every external link. Treat those claims as hypotheses to test, not laws.

## Craft principles vs. ranking guesses

| Craft principle (helps readers) | Ranking guess (treat as a hypothesis) |
|---|---|
| Short paragraphs make a dense argument easier to scan | "Short lines get boosted" |
| A personal scene makes an abstract issue concrete | "Stories always go viral" |
| A clear opening helps the reader decide to continue | "The first line is all the algorithm reads" |
| Put a link where it best serves the reader | "Links always kill reach" |

**External links** are a good example of confused reasoning. A link asks readers to leave the feed, so posts with links may perform differently. That alone doesn't prove an algorithmic penalty. Put the link where it serves the reader, test for your own objective, and never bury an essential resource just to obey folklore.

## Give good work a realistic route to readers

Distribution starts before you publish:

- **Connect with people whose work you can contribute to.** A smaller network with shared professional context beats a large pile of unrelated contacts. Don't send connection requests that hide a prepared pitch. (That's the whole subject of *The Non-Salesy DM*.)
- **Comment like a contributor.** Add an example, a useful distinction or a well-formed question. "Great post!" repeated across the feed gives nobody a reason to look at your work.
- **Make posts easy to pass along privately.** Ask: *who would send this to whom, and what sentence would they write when they did?* "Our team needs to decide this before the launch" is a much stronger distribution reason than "this person wants reach."
- **Use your existing channels honestly.** Reference a relevant post in your newsletter or in an agreed client follow-up. Never ask a group to simulate independent enthusiasm. That's a pod, and pods are exactly what LinkedIn says it acts against.
- **Collaborate with substance.** A joint post between a sales-process consultant and an agency operations lead can expose trade-offs neither would explain alone. Agree the topic, permissions and credit first.

**For new accounts:** the first problem is usually limited exposure, not weak writing. Spend part of your first month finding where your buyers already discuss the problem, and participate usefully there. Don't treat low early reach as proof you need a more sensational hook.

## Build your own small evidence base

Your best data is your own. Review ten of your posts with a similar audience and purpose. Record topic, format, opening, proof, CTA, reach, **relevant** responses and opportunities. Look for patterns, then test one change across several comparable posts.

Don't call a three-post difference conclusive. And if you're starting from zero, build a baseline instead of borrowing someone else's. Those early conversations are research even when the analytics look flat.

## Action: audit one "rule"

Pick one popular LinkedIn writing rule you currently follow. Write down:

1. The claim
2. Its source
3. An alternative explanation for why it might seem to work
4. A practical test

Replace "LinkedIn always rewards this" with "I'll test whether this helps my readers and my objective."

# Chapter 3: The Anatomy of a Useful Post

<p class="lede">Hook → Rehook → Body → Payoff → CTA.</p>

Every useful LinkedIn post has a coherent movement. These are **functions**, not boxes of equal size. A short post may combine several in one sentence. A case study may need room to set up context before its lesson is believable.

<div class="flow">
<span><b>Hook</b><br>Stop the right reader</span><span><b>Rehook</b><br>Make the next line necessary</span><span><b>Body</b><br>Deliver the story or value</span><span><b>Payoff</b><br>Resolve the promise</span><span><b>CTA</b><br>A proportionate next step</span>
</div>
<p class="fig">Figure 2. The Attention Architecture, the structure used throughout this book.</p>

When one part contradicts another (a big hook with a thin body, a helpful post with a pushy CTA), the post feels manipulative even if every sentence is polished.

## A complete miniature

<div class="post" markdown="1">
Your proposal may be waiting for a decision nobody owns.

Before rewriting the pricing page, check what happened after the last call.

Was one person responsible for taking the recommendation to their team? Was there a date for that conversation? Did you agree what the other decision-makers would need?

"They liked the proposal" is a reaction.
"The ops lead reviews scope with the founder on Thursday" is a next step.

On your next sales call, finish by confirming the decision, the owner and the date. If any of them is unknown, write that down instead of treating the deal as close to won.

Which of those three is usually missing in your pipeline?
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Function | What it does here |
|---|---|
| **Hook** | Names a specific, recognizable failure |
| **Rehook** | Redirects from the familiar response (rewrite the price) to the real check |
| **Body** | Three questions the reader can run on their own pipeline |
| **Payoff** | A memorable contrast (reaction vs. next step) plus one concrete habit |
| **CTA** | Invites relevant experience, not agreement |
</div>

## The promise–payoff contract

Before drafting, write the promise in plain language: *"This post will help an agency owner tell a pricing objection apart from an unclear decision process."* Then check every paragraph against it.

- If the hook promises a pricing diagnosis and the body turns into a speech about confidence, the contract is broken.
- If the hook promises five steps, deliver five usable steps.
- If it implies measured results, show the measurement context.

**Never withhold the promised answer to force a DM.** Give the insight publicly. Offer a resource or service that makes *implementation* easier, broader or more tailored. Readers can tell the difference between a post that teaches and a post that teases, and only one of them builds authority.

## V.I.R.A.L. inside the post

The architecture tells you the order. V.I.R.A.L. tells you what each part must contain:

| | Question to ask | Lives mostly in… |
|---|---|---|
| **Voice** | Does this sound like a person who understands the work? | Everywhere |
| **Intrigue** | Would the right reader need the next line? | Hook, rehook |
| **Relevance** | Would my buyer recognize themselves? | Hook, body |
| **Authority** | What here gives them grounds to believe me? | Body |
| **Lesson** | What can they now do or decide? | Payoff |

After the post, **L.E.A.D.** takes over (Chapters 13–17): create a reason to continue (**Lead**), start a real exchange (**Engagement**), check fit (**Alignment**), and move the right people into a business conversation (**Discovery**).

## Match the format to the reader's task

- **Text** for an argument, a short story or a focused explanation.
- **A simple image** for a comparison or diagnostic people grasp at a glance.
- **A document (carousel) post** when a sequence benefits from separate pages.
- **Video** when a demonstration or spoken explanation is materially clearer.

These are communication choices, not rankings of algorithmic superiority. Design for legibility: one main idea per visual, readable labels, real contrast. A tiny spreadsheet screenshot isn't proof if nobody can read it.

## Action: five sentences first

Write one sentence for each of the five functions. Expand only the body until it delivers the promised lesson. Then read the hook and the payoff one after the other. They should make a sensible pair on their own.

<div class="rule">Field rule: The hook makes a promise. The body keeps it. Don't write checks the post can't cash.</div>
