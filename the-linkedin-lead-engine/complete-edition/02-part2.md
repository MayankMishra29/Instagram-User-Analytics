# Chapter 4: The Hook

<p class="lede">Win the first two seconds with the right reader, not every reader.</p>

The opening line has one job: help a suitable reader recognize either **their situation** or **an interesting implication**. It doesn't need to shout louder than every other post. It needs to make the next sentence worth the right person's effort.

A hook needs three things:

1. **A subject:** what this is about.
2. **A reason to care:** why it matters to someone.
3. **Enough specificity** not to sound interchangeable.

"Marketing has changed" has a subject and nothing else. "Your demo-request form may be filtering out the buyers with real urgency" has a problem, a mechanism and an obvious person who should care.

## Eleven ways to open

| Type | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem** | Names an observable difficulty | "Your proposals go quiet after procurement joins." |
| **Curiosity** | Opens a meaningful gap | "The busiest stage in your CRM may be the least understood." |
| **Contrarian** | Challenges a belief, with a boundary | "More discovery questions can make a simple purchase harder." |
| **Personal story** | Starts at the decision or consequence | "I rewrote the offer before checking who was reading it." |
| **Transformation** | Contrasts a real before and after | "The handoff went from a Slack message to a named owner and deadline." |
| **How I** | Reveals a process you actually use | "How I turn one buyer objection into three useful posts." |
| **Mistake** | Identifies a correctable action | "A common proposal mistake: explaining scope before confirming who decides." |
| **Confession** | Admits a relevant misconception | "I used to treat every resource request as a sales lead." |
| **Data** | Leads with a supported finding | "In our last 40 inquiries, one question came up 23 times." *(only with real, sourced numbers)* |
| **Prediction** | A reasoned forecast with conditions | "If buyers can compare your process before a call, discovery calls need a new job." |
| **Direct address** | Names the reader and circumstance | "Agency founders who still approve every proposal: read this before hiring another salesperson." |

### From my own feed

Here are openings from posts I've written, so you can see these types in a real voice:

- **Problem / reframe:** *"Most freelancers don't have an income problem. They have a pipeline problem."*
- **Contrarian:** *"We chase reach as if it were the finish line, then wonder where the revenue went."*
- **Scene:** *"'Can we just have a quick call?' Every consultant has heard this one."*
- **How I:** *"Here's how I'd market a startup with a $0 ad budget…"*
- **Confession (with humor):** *"Nothing keeps a growth marketer humble like a dashboard."*
- **Research:** *"I analyzed 100+ AI tools, consultant workflows, founder recommendations and AI communities to answer one question…"*
- **Comparison:** *"There are 2 types of AI consultants on our platform right now. (And only one of them consistently gets businesses interested.)"*
- **Personal story:** *"If you only saw my job titles, you'd miss the story."*

Notice that each one tells a specific reader, like a freelancer, a consultant or a founder, that this post is for them.

## The 15-word test

Try expressing your hook's core idea in 15 words or fewer. This is a compression exercise, not a LinkedIn rule. If the shorter version drops an essential qualifier or becomes misleading, keep the qualifier.

**Draft ten hooks before choosing one.** Vary the *angle*, not the adjectives: the symptom, the consequence, the decision, the common response, the overlooked constraint, the evidence, the misconception, an example, a question, a practical promise. Then pick the one most clearly connected to the body.

## Repair the overclaim

| Weak | Better | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "This one trick will double your leads." | "Before posting more, check whether buyers can tell what you sell." | A smaller promise you can actually deliver |
| "Nobody talks about this sales secret." | "A signed proposal still needs a clear handoff into delivery." | Removes fake exclusivity; makes the issue concrete |
| "I almost lost everything." | "I sent the proposal before confirming who would approve it." | True and relevant beats dramatic and vague |

A hook that needs exaggeration to stay interesting is telling you the idea underneath is weak. Strengthen the idea.

## Action: ten hooks, two finalists

For your next post, write ten hooks across different angles. Score each as clear / partly clear / unclear on three things: **audience**, **problem** and **deliverability** (can the body keep this promise?). Discard any that need exaggeration. Take two finalists to one person in your audience and ask what they expect the post to explain.

<div class="rule">Field rule: A great hook makes the right reader stop and the wrong reader keep scrolling. Both are wins.</div>

# Chapter 5: The Rehook

<p class="lede">Most people obsess over line one. Experts engineer lines one to three as a unit.</p>

The **rehook** is the sentence or short passage right after the hook that makes the opening worth continuing. On LinkedIn it usually decides whether someone taps "…see more." It's one of the most under-taught skills in post writing.

<div class="flow">
<span>Hook</span><span>Tension</span><span>Curiosity</span><span>"…see more"</span>
</div>

A good rehook **adds information**. Repeating the hook in more dramatic language only delays the answer.

<div class="post" markdown="1">
I almost quit my business.

Not because it was failing.

Because it was succeeding in the wrong way.
</div>

The second and third lines turn a generic confession into a specific, intriguing tension: *what does succeeding the wrong way mean?* Now "see more" is an informed choice. (Use this shape only for a true story.)

Consider: "Your marketing report can improve while your pipeline gets weaker." A useful rehook: "That happens when the dashboard rewards activity your buyers don't use to decide." It names a mechanism. "You won't believe what happens next" adds nothing.

## Three dependable moves

- **Narrow:** "This matters most when the founder still approves every proposal." It helps the right reader recognize themselves, and lets everyone else leave without wasting their time.
- **Complicate:** "The team may be following the process exactly. The process may be missing the decision." It earns attention by improving the diagnosis, not by adding suspense.
- **Preview:** "Here are three places to check before you change your price." It sets an honest expectation and a clear scope.

You can combine them. But don't build a five-line runway for a two-line insight. If the opening takes longer than the lesson, compress.

## Design for the visible opening, without gaming it

How much of a post shows before "see more" varies by device, format and interface, so don't assume a fixed number of lines. Make your first short passage make sense on its own, and preview it in the interface you publish from.

**Don't withhold the entire topic.** A mystery opening earns curiosity from people who have no reason to care, and frustrates the ones who do. The rehook should give enough context that clicking "see more" is an informed yes.

## Worked repair

- **Draft:** "I made a huge mistake. It cost me everything. Here's what happened." Unless every word is true and relevant, this is inflated and empty.
- **Revision (true experience):** "I sent the proposal before confirming who would approve it. The next two calls repeated a conversation I thought we'd finished." Now the reader knows the situation, the consequence and the likely lesson.
- **Revision (no personal story available):** "Sending a proposal before you know who approves it creates an avoidable risk: you may be presenting the answer to someone who still needs help framing the problem." That's an argument, not a fabricated memory. Both work.

## Action: three rehooks per hook

For your chosen hook, write three rehooks: one that **narrows**, one that **complicates** and one that **previews**. Pick the one that adds the most meaning. Read the hook and rehook aloud together, and cut any line that could be pasted under twenty unrelated hooks.

<div class="rule">Field rule: The hook earns a glance. The rehook earns the read.</div>

# Chapter 6: The Seven Post Engines

<p class="lede">Choose the engine by your material, not your mood.</p>

An **engine** is a reusable way of organizing a useful idea. It cuts the number of decisions you need to make before you start writing. It doesn't replace the idea, the evidence or the audience. And the same engine can attract, build authority or convert, depending on its subject and next step.

<div class="table-caption">The Seven Engines at a Glance</div>

| Engine | Use when you have… | Structure | Lead potential |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Story** | A real sequence of events that clarifies a decision | Situation → tension → choice → consequence → interpretation | High: shows how you think in situations buyers recognize |
| **2. How-to** | A bounded task the reader can perform | Outcome → prerequisites → steps → quality check → limit | High: shows usefulness; leads naturally to implementation help |
| **3. List** | Distinct signals, mistakes or checks | Unifying rule → genuinely different items → priority | Medium–high: great for diagnostics |
| **4. Contrarian** | A familiar recommendation that fails under clear conditions | Common advice → missing assumption → counterexample → better rule → boundary | High: signals judgment |
| **5. Trend** | A verified current change affecting a buyer decision | Source → what changed → implication → what to check | Medium: timely, but perishable |
| **6. Lessons learned** | A genuine change in how you think | Old belief → evidence → revision → application | High: credible and human |
| **7. Conversation** | A real open question for people with experience | Context → your current view → specific question | Medium: builds relationships and research |

## Engine 1 · Story

Use a story when a real sequence of events makes a decision easier to understand. The lead potential comes from showing how you think, in circumstances your buyers recognize.

*Example:* a genuine account of discovering that a delayed project had no internal owner: what you first assumed, what changed your diagnosis, what you did differently. Don't stretch one incident into a universal law.

**Template:** "We expected [outcome]. Then [observable event] happened. We considered [options] and chose [action] because [constraint]. The result was [supported outcome]. The lesson applies when [boundary]."

## Engine 2 · How-to

Use a how-to when the reader needs to complete a bounded task.

*Example:* "Before your next proposal, write down the decision owner, the review date and the evidence each reviewer needs. Confirm the unknowns on the call. Put the agreed next action in your recap." Specific enough to try today.

**Template:** "To [task], first check [prerequisite]. Do [step], then [step]. You'll know it's ready when [test]. If [constraint], use [alternative] instead."

## Engine 3 · List

Use a list for distinct items with a unifying rule. Don't split one idea into ten near-duplicates to make the headline look substantial.

*Example:* Three signs your lead magnet is attracting the wrong audience: most requests come from peers outside your market; follow-up questions are about a different problem; people can't say when they'd use the resource. Each one points to a different repair.

**Template:** "[Number] signs of [specific condition]. [Signal] means check [cause]. Start with [priority rule]."

## Engine 4 · Contrarian

Use this when a popular recommendation breaks under identifiable conditions. Its commercial value comes from better judgment, not controversy.

*Example:* "Post every day" is poor advice for a consultant whose best material comes from delivery work but whose schedule can't support thoughtful daily writing. Three strong posts a week may produce better material *and* leave time to answer buyers.

**Template:** "[Advice] makes sense when [condition]. It breaks down when [constraint]. In that situation, choose [alternative] because [mechanism]."

## Engine 5 · Trend

Use a trend only when a current change affects a decision your audience has to make. Verify the source. Separate what happened from what you predict. Don't summarize the announcement. Translate it into a decision.

**Template:** "[Source] announced [change] on [date]. For [reader], the practical question is [decision]. Check [factor] before [action]. My expectation is [inference], especially if [condition]."

## Engine 6 · Lessons learned

Use this when you can honestly compare your old model with your current one. It's often more credible than presenting yourself as someone who never got anything wrong.

*Example:* learning that a polished dashboard doesn't resolve conflicting definitions of a "qualified lead," and that you now agree the definitions before building any report.

**Template:** "I used to prioritize [choice]. After [real evidence], I now check [factor] first. That changes [action]. I still use the old approach when [exception]."

## Engine 7 · Conversation

Use a conversation post to investigate a real question with people who have relevant experience. Give enough context to make a good answer possible, share your own view, and say what would change it.

*Example:* "For agency owners with a dedicated account manager: which decisions still reach you every week? I'm especially curious about scope changes versus client escalations."

**Template:** "In [context], I see a choice between [A] and [B]. My current view is [reasoned position]. What have you seen when [specific condition]?"

## Choose by material

| If you have… | Use the… |
|---|---|
| A sequence of events | Story |
| A repeatable task | How-to |
| A cluster of distinct signals | List |
| A flawed popular assumption | Contrarian |
| A verified change | Trend |
| A revised belief | Lessons learned |
| An open question | Conversation |

## Action: one problem, seven premises

Take one buyer problem and write a one-sentence premise for all seven engines. Choose the two where your material is strongest. Save the rest as research questions rather than forcing seven thin posts into your calendar.

<div class="rule">Field rule: The engine organizes the idea. It can't replace one.</div>

# Chapter 7: Write Like a Person Who Understands the Work

<p class="lede">Voice isn't slang. It's specificity about people, decisions and consequences.</p>

"Human" writing doesn't mean casual slang, deliberately broken grammar or a personal confession in every opening. A precise explanation often sounds more human than a manufactured story.

Corporate abstraction hides the work. "We empower scalable stakeholder alignment" tells the reader nothing. "Before the project starts, we agree who approves changes and how fast they need to respond" describes something a buyer can picture, and evaluate.

## Edit at three levels

- **Idea:** What changes for the reader after reading this?
- **Paragraph:** What does each paragraph contribute? Cut any that don't.
- **Sentence:** Replace vague subjects and verbs with concrete ones.

**Before:** "It is important to ensure that effective communication is prioritized."
**After:** "Send the client a weekly note: work completed, decisions blocked, next deadline."

The second version can be acted on, and challenged. That's what makes it credible.

**Rhythm:** short sentences land a decision. Longer ones show a condition or relationship. A post made entirely of clipped one-line declarations becomes monotonous and theatrical. Mix them.

## Strong views, honest boundaries

"Always," "never" and "everyone" usually mean the draft has outrun the evidence. Replace them with the actual conditions. "A weekly review helps when several people depend on the same decision" is less dramatic than "Every company needs this meeting," and far more useful.

You can hold a strong point of view without pretending there are no exceptions: state the recommendation, explain why, and name the condition that would change it. That matters most when your reader knows their own business better than you do.

## Use AI as an editor, not an author

Give your assistant the audience, the thesis, your source notes, the facts you're allowed to use and a sample of your voice. Ask it to find vague claims, missing steps and unsupported numbers *before* you ask for a rewrite. And explicitly prohibit invented clients, experiences, quotes and results:

> "Preserve these facts. Flag anything that needs evidence. Suggest three clearer openings. Do not add experiences, clients or results."

You're still responsible for the final draft. A fluent paragraph can misrepresent a source or imply a result you never achieved. Keep confidential client information out of tools that aren't approved for it. Anonymizing a name doesn't help if the project, dates and figures are distinctive. (The AI Post Coach in the Implementation System is built around exactly these rules.)

## Action: the abstraction hunt

Highlight every abstract noun in a draft. Replace at least five with an actor, an action or an observable result. Then delete any sentence that praises the idea without explaining it.

<div class="rule">Field rule: If a buyer can't picture it, they can't buy it.</div>

# Chapter 8: Stories That Help Buyers Decide

<p class="lede">A story earns its place when the event teaches something relevant.</p>

The reader doesn't need your biography. They need enough context to understand the choice, enough tension to care and enough evidence to trust your interpretation.

**Start near the decision.** "The project was approved, but nobody could name who was responsible for system access" is much closer to the problem than a paragraph about your passion for operational excellence.

## The decision story

Build any story from five questions:

1. What was supposed to happen?
2. What made it difficult?
3. What options existed?
4. Why was one chosen?
5. What changed, and what's still uncertain?

This exposes your **judgment**, which is what buyers are really evaluating. It avoids the common pattern where the author appears, applies a mysterious framework and produces a remarkable result without ever explaining the work.

<div class="post" markdown="1">
The team wanted a faster proposal template.

But the template was already short. The delay happened before anyone opened it.

Every non-standard request went to the founder. Nobody knew which changes an account lead could approve.

A better template might save writing time. It wouldn't remove the approval queue.

The first repair was a decision rule: which changes the account lead can approve, which need review, and what the reviewer needs to see.

Before speeding up a document, check the decision that comes before it.
</div>
<p class="fig">Illustrative story. It demonstrates a mechanism without claiming a real client result.</p>

## Protect the evidence chain

For real cases, keep the starting state, what you did, the time period, how you defined the outcome, and anything else that changed. If inquiries rose while pricing *and* paid distribution also changed, don't credit the content rewrite alone. If a client gave you a number you didn't verify, attribute it to them.

**Ask permission** before using client names, screenshots, quotes or distinctive details. Without permission, use a clearly labeled composite or a generic process example. Never quietly merge several clients into one apparently real success story.

## Emotion belongs in the consequence

Frustration, uncertainty, relief and pride help readers understand why a decision mattered, but the emotion should come from the situation, not from declarations that a routine mistake "destroyed everything." For a founder, being the only person who can approve work means interrupted evenings. For an operations lead, a vague handoff means taking the blame for a delay they couldn't prevent. Those stakes are believable because they're attached to a real workflow.

## Action: a 200-word decision story

Write a story about a real professional decision in 200 words. Underline the evidence. Circle the interpretation. Add one sentence about where your lesson might *not* apply. If fact and interpretation blur together, revise before publishing.

<div class="rule">Field rule: Buyers don't hire the hero of a story. They hire the judgment inside it.</div>
