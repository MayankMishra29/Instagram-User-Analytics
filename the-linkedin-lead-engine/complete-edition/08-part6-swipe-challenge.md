# Chapter 25: The Viral-to-Lead Swipe File

<p class="lede">Annotated examples: hook, rehook, structure, trigger, audience, value, CTA and lead mechanism.</p>

The first seven are **original worked examples** written for this book. They aren't reproductions of anyone's posts, and their performance hasn't been measured. "Viral-to-lead" names the intended path, not a claim that they went viral. After them, I annotate openings from **my own feed**.

Read each one twice: first as the intended buyer, then as the writer studying the choices.

---

### Example 1 · Agency founder dependence

<div class="post" markdown="1">
Your sales process has an availability problem if every proposal waits for you.

A new template won't decide which exceptions your team can approve.

Start with the last proposal that stalled while you were away. What was the team waiting to find out?

Whether the price could change?
Whether the scope was deliverable?
Whether the client was a good fit?

Those are three different decisions. They need three different rules.

Write one boundary for the most common request: who can approve it, within what limits, and when it comes back to you.

Then test that rule on your next real proposal.

Which decision reaches you most often?
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Element | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Hook** | Selects founders who experience proposal delays; reframes it as *availability* |
| **Rehook** | Separates document design from decision rights |
| **Structure** | Diagnostic list → small test |
| **Trigger** | Recognition of a familiar interruption |
| **Audience / job** | Agency founders · authority |
| **CTA** | Experience: invites real operating context |
| **Lead mechanism** | A founder describing repeated scope escalation may be a fit for process work. Answer their situation first. Someone just agreeing isn't an opportunity. |
</div>

### Example 2 · SaaS onboarding

<div class="post" markdown="1">
A welcome email can't fix an unclear first task.

If a new user opens the product and still has to figure out what success means, more reminders just repeat the confusion.

Check the first useful action.

Can the user name it? Do they have what they need to complete it? Does the product show what changed when they're done?

For a reporting tool, that might mean connecting one data source and seeing one meaningful report. For a collaboration tool, it may require a second person to join.

The right first action depends on your product's value, not on how easy it is to count the click.

Before rewriting your email sequence, describe the first useful task in one sentence a new user would understand.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Element | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Hook** | Challenges an overly narrow fix |
| **Rehook** | Explains the mechanism |
| **Structure** | Diagnostic questions + contrasting examples |
| **Audience / job** | SaaS founders, product and lifecycle teams · authority |
| **CTA** | Application: no lead capture at all |
| **Lead mechanism** | The profile carries the onboarding service. A reader asking *how to define the first useful task* can lead to a scoped conversation. No activation result is claimed. |
</div>

### Example 3 · Specialist freelancer positioning

<div class="post" markdown="1">
"I can write anything" makes a buyer do too much work.

They still need to know whether you understand the document they're trying to produce.

A SaaS founder who needs a customer story needs interviews, evidence, approvals and a narrative that helps a buyer evaluate the product.

A founder who needs weekly LinkedIn posts needs a completely different process.

You may be great at both. Your service page should make each one easy to evaluate.

Name the reader, the deliverable, the inputs and what a good result looks like. Show one relevant sample.

Specificity helps because it reduces the buyer's translation work.

Open your profile. Can a stranger identify one document you're especially good at producing?
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Element | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Hook** | Names a positioning habit in the reader's own words |
| **Rehook** | Names the hidden burden it puts on the buyer |
| **Structure** | Problem → comparison → decision rule → application |
| **Trigger** | Self-recognition, not spectacle |
| **Audience / job** | Specialist freelancers · attraction + authority |
| **Lead mechanism** | A positioning consultant could connect this to a profile review, but the post is useful without it. |
</div>

### Example 4 · Interview coaching, without inflated promises

<div class="post" markdown="1">
Right after an interview, you have a narrow window to capture useful evidence.

"It went badly" is a feeling. It isn't a diagnosis yet.

Write down the questions you remember. Note where you gave a clear example, where you got vague and where the interviewer asked you to clarify.

Then separate preparation gaps from information you simply didn't have.

Couldn't explain a project decision? Practice that explanation. Role needed a skill you haven't used? Decide whether to build it or target a different role.

You can't read the hiring decision from every facial expression.

Use this interview to improve the next one, without pretending you control the employer's choice.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Element | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Hook** | A timely task with a deadline built in |
| **Rehook** | Replaces an unhelpful judgment with a diagnostic need |
| **Structure** | Reflection method + boundary |
| **Audience / job** | Job candidates · authority |
| **CTA** | None: the lesson is the ending |
| **Lead mechanism** | A related post could offer the reflection sheet. A coaching offer must avoid promising job outcomes the coach can't control. |
</div>

### Example 5 · A resource post with clear fit

<div class="post" markdown="1">
Before adding another sales meeting, check which decision the current one fails to make.

A longer agenda isn't the same as a clearer outcome.

Take one recurring meeting and write down four things: the decision required, the person responsible, the evidence needed and the deadline.

No decision? It may be an update that belongs in writing. Decision with no owner? Inviting more people may make it worse.

I put those four fields into a one-page meeting review sheet for service-business team leads.

It helps you redesign one recurring meeting. It doesn't prescribe your whole management system.

It's at the link below. No email required.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Element | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Hook + rehook** | Make a distinction the body then explains |
| **Value delivered publicly** | The four fields: usable with or without the download |
| **Resource promise** | Bounded ("one meeting") with an explicit limit |
| **Access** | Stated clearly (no email) |
| **Lead mechanism** | Requests to implement decision ownership across teams may indicate consulting fit. Downloads alone don't. |
</div>

### Example 6 · A transparent commercial post

<div class="post" markdown="1">
If proposal delays keep pulling you back into sales, a focused process review can show you why.

It covers qualification, proposal prep, decision ownership and follow-up.

We start with a handful of recent opportunities and map where work waited, what information was missing, and who could have moved it forward.

You get a prioritized list of changes, each with an owner and a suggested testing order.

It's for service businesses with an existing offer and an active sales process. It's not a substitute for validating demand from scratch.

You'll need to share suitable records and involve whoever owns sales operations.

If you want the scope, timeline and fee, message me about a proposal-process review.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Element | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Hook** | Selects by problem, not by title |
| **Rehook** | Names the scope immediately |
| **Body** | Method, output, fit, non-fit, participation |
| **CTA** | Explicitly commercial, with a clear keyword for the DM |
| **Lead mechanism** | Reply with actual scope, timing and price, and don't make them repeat details they already gave you. Use only for a service you can really deliver. |
</div>

### Example 7 · A relationship post with a real question

<div class="post" markdown="1">
Account leads: which client requests can you approve without checking with the founder?

I'm interested in the boundary, not the job title.

A team can have experienced people and still route every small scope change through one person. Sometimes that protects margin. Sometimes the limits have just never been written down.

One useful distinction: a change that fits existing capacity vs. one that changes the delivery commitment.

My starting point would be a short list of changes the account lead can approve, with exceptions reviewed together.

Where does that break down in your team? No client names or sensitive numbers needed.
</div>

<div class="annot" markdown="1">
| Element | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Hook** | Narrows the respondent group precisely |
| **Body** | Gives a reasoned starting view so respondents aren't doing all the thinking |
| **CTA** | Invites counterexamples; explicitly protects confidentiality |
| **Lead mechanism** | The job is relationship and research. A reply should *not* trigger a pitch. If someone asks for implementation help, evaluate it then. |
</div>

## From my own feed: three openings, annotated

These are real openings from posts I've published. I'm showing them for their structure, not as proof of performance.

<div class="post" markdown="1">
We chase reach as if it were the finish line, then wonder where the revenue went.

Attention gets the credit, but the funnel does the work.
</div>

**Why it's built this way:** a contrarian hook aimed at founders and marketers who over-index on reach. The rehook ("attention gets the credit, but the funnel does the work") names the mechanism in one line, which is this book's thesis compressed. The post then used an analogy ("a viral post is a crowd that gathers for ten seconds") to make the abstract concrete.

<div class="post" markdown="1">
Most freelancers don't have an income problem.
They have a pipeline problem.

They rely on one source for clients. When that source dries up, so does their income.
</div>

**Why it's built this way:** a problem-reframe hook (#06 in the hook library). It names the reader (freelancers), replaces their framing ("income") with a more useful diagnosis ("pipeline"), and the rehook gives the mechanism in two short sentences. That problem territory sits right next to a service: helping freelancers build a multi-channel pipeline.

<div class="post" markdown="1">
"Can we just have a quick call?"

Every consultant has heard this one.

A client books 15 minutes. 15 becomes 30. 30 becomes 45. And somewhere along the way, that "quick call" turns into a full-blown strategy session.
</div>

**Why it's built this way:** a scene hook using the reader's own everyday language. Instant recognition for consultants. The escalating numbers work as a rehook: the reader feels the problem before any lesson arrives. It's a relationship-and-authority post: it builds rapport through shared experience, then earns the right to teach something about scoping and paid advice.

## Build your own swipe

Pick one example. Replace its audience, problem, evidence and next step with yours. Label each function in your draft (hook, rehook, body, payoff, CTA). If you can't point to the value delivered *before* the CTA, revise the body. If your authority depends on a result you haven't achieved, switch to a transparent demonstration.

# Conclusion: Stop Going Viral. Start Becoming Unmissable.

A lead engine is a sequence of credible experiences. The post makes a problem clearer. The profile confirms your relevance. The resource helps with a real task. The conversation respects context. The offer explains the work. Delivery keeps the promise.

You can improve every one of those experiences without predicting your next viral post. **That's where your control lives.**

You don't need millions of followers to generate serious business from LinkedIn. I don't have them. You need the ability to consistently earn attention from the right people, show that you understand their problems, and give them a good reason to start a conversation.

So start small and specific. One audience. One problem worth understanding. Write from evidence. Make the opening clear, the body useful and the next step appropriate. Pay attention to what readers reveal. When interest becomes a real opportunity, evaluate it honestly. When it doesn't, leave the person with something useful anyway.

Don't try to become a LinkedIn influencer.

**Become the person your ideal buyers already trust before they ever speak to you.**

Your next action is concrete: complete the buyer and offer brief, choose one problem, and draft a post that helps someone make a better decision today.
