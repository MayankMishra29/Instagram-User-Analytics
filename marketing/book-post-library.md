# The Non-Salesy DM

## NSDM-01: The trust tax
**Source:** Ch. 3 · **CTA:** Field Guide (TRUST)

"Adding value" is one of the most common ways to kill a deal in the DMs.

Here's why.

Every extra thing you send hands the buyer one more small uncertainty to resolve:

→ a second link
→ a wall of text
→ a vague offer
→ an unexpected call request
→ a promise that sounds too big

I call it the trust tax. Each item raises the value of your message a little, and raises its complexity and obligation a lot.

A prospect says: "I get what you do. Not sure it's worth taking on right now."

The usual move is to send another testimonial. The prospect goes quiet.

The testimonial answered "can this work?" The real issue was risk: the team had no capacity that month. One question would have found it:

"When you say it may not be worth it right now, is the doubt about the result, your team's workload, or something else?"

Don't add value to compensate for risk. Remove the risk.

## NSDM-02: Price is information
**Source:** Ch. 11 · **CTA:** Complete Edition · Paid-only material

The buyer asks what it costs. The seller sends a booking link.

This is one of the most common trust leaks in B2B selling, and we treat it like a tactic.

Hiding the price to protect the meeting gets you a meeting booked through avoidable uncertainty. That's weak progress.

When you know your price, state it. A complete answer has:

→ the amount and currency
→ one-time or recurring
→ what it covers
→ what it doesn't

"For the four-week, one-team pilot we discussed: $3,000, one-time. That covers the process map, the brief and a handover session. Company-wide rollout isn't included." (An illustrative figure. Use one you can deliver profitably.)

If scope really changes the price, name what drives it and the smallest useful next step.

"It depends" is not a complete answer.

## NSDM-03: "I need to think about it"
**Source:** Ch. 10 + 13 · **CTA:** Field Guide (TRUST)

"I need to think about it" can mean at least eight different things:

1. They don't believe it'll work for them.
2. It feels risky.
3. Information is missing.
4. Someone internally disagrees.
5. The timing is wrong.
6. There's no urgency.
7. They genuinely want to think.
8. It's a polite no.

Most sellers answer all eight the same way: a follow-up in three days with "just checking in."

Diagnose before you respond. One question is enough:

"Of course. Is there an open question I can help with, or would you rather have some time with it?"

Then:
→ If they name a concern, address that concern, not the one you're used to hearing.
→ If they want time, agree when to reconnect.
→ If they decline, stop.

Clarify once. A refusal is not a discovery opportunity.

## NSDM-04: The four kinds of quiet
**Source:** Ch. 10 · **CTA:** Complete Edition · Paid-only material

Not all silence in your DMs is the same.

There are four kinds of quiet, and each needs a different response:

Dormant trust: real engagement, then quiet. They may still be reading your posts.
→ Leave it. Reopen when there's a genuinely relevant trigger.

Polite interest: courteous replies, no substance, no questions.
→ Answer what they asked, then let it rest.

Active exploration: questions about their situation, then a pause, often while they evaluate internally.
→ One proportionate check-in tied to what's open.

Disengagement: a clear decline, or repeated non-response with nothing open.
→ Close the record.

The automated sequence treats all four the same: another nudge on day 3, day 7 and day 14.

That's how you convert dormant trust into disengagement.

Silence is data. It just isn't one data point.

## NSDM-05: The Two-Hypothesis Reply
**Source:** Ch. 4 · **CTA:** Complete Edition · Paid-only material

The most useful habit I know for ambiguous DMs takes 10 seconds.

When a reply could mean two things:

1. Write down the two most likely states.
2. Choose a move that works under both.

"Interesting, send me more."

Hypothesis A: they're actively evaluating.
Hypothesis B: they're politely wrapping up.

A short, relevant answer to what they asked works for both.
A surprise call request works for A and adds friction to B.

Another one:

Rep: "The workshop helps teams separate two offers buyers confuse."
Buyer: "Interesting. We have a few overlaps."
Rep: "Do buyers struggle to choose, or is it mainly confusing inside the team?"
Buyer: "Inside the team. Customers seem fine."

That answer just told the rep their customer-facing workshop may be the wrong fix.

The wrong next line is "Exactly, that's why you need our workshop."

A correction is information, not resistance to overcome.

## NSDM-06: Rehydration is not follow-up
**Source:** Ch. 14 · **CTA:** Complete Edition · Paid-only material

"It's been 90 days, let's check in" is not a reason to message someone.

Old threads aren't dead. But reopening one needs a real basis:

Good reasons:
→ They invited it: "Come back after the launch."
→ Something material changed that connects to what you discussed.

Bad reasons:
→ Your pipeline is empty.
→ A generic interval passed.
→ They liked an unrelated post.
→ They said no.

A good rehydration message names what you actually discussed, asks one status question, and is easy to ignore.

"You asked me to revisit the website after the product launch. Is the team considering that work now, or is it still on hold?"

And if the answer is "still on hold":

"Understood. I'll close this on my side rather than add reminders. You're welcome to pick it up when the timing changes."

An invitation to revisit once isn't an invitation to pursue forever. Closing cleanly is a trust deposit.

## NSDM-07: Signal lag
**Source:** Ch. 10 · **CTA:** Bundle

Someone can read your posts for months, visit your profile twice, remember one idea and never tell you.

Then one day: "Do you still run that workshop?"

That delay between trust forming and trust showing up as action is signal lag.

It teaches two things at once:

1. No immediate action doesn't mean no trust. Many buyers build memory quietly. Nothing you DMed them in the meantime caused their message. The content did the work and the profile confirmed it.

2. Signal lag is not proof of hidden intent. A profile view can be curiosity, a misclick or a hiring check. Don't use the idea to treat every silent viewer as a lead you're entitled to chase.

This is why content and DMs are one system. Content builds memory at scale. DMs handle the moment someone is ready.

## NSDM-08: Objections are gate reports
**Source:** Ch. 13 · **CTA:** Complete Edition · Paid-only material

An objection isn't an obstacle. It's the buyer telling you which question is still open.

"Send me some information."
→ Real evaluation, or a gentle exit. Send something concise. Don't make a call the price of basic information.

"We already work with someone."
→ "Makes sense. If they cover this well, there may be no reason to change. Is the handoff part of their scope?"

"We tried this before."
→ "What did it change, and where did it break down? I want to check my approach would be materially different before suggesting another try."

"Can you do a free audit first?"
→ Offer one or two public observations. Be clear that real diagnosis is paid work.

"Not interested."
→ "Understood, thanks for letting me know. I won't follow up on this." Then stop.

Two rules above all of it:
1. Clarify once. One respectful question, not a sequence of rebuttals.
2. A refusal isn't a discovery opportunity. They owe you no reason.

## NSDM-09: Hiding is a failure too
**Source:** Ch. 1 · **CTA:** Field Guide (TRUST)

Everyone warns you about being pushy in DMs.

Nobody warns you about the opposite.

You've read the "don't be salesy" advice. So now when a buyer asks "How would you solve this for us?", you send a helpful article.

They ask about pricing. You suggest a call "to understand their needs first."

That's not patience. It's hiding.

Ambiguity is a risk to the buyer, and risk stops decisions.

Pushing moves faster than the buyer. Hiding moves slower. Both ignore where the buyer actually is.

The goal was never "level 0 human" forever. Real deals need commercial clarity:
→ It's clear that business exists.
→ They're free to decline.
→ When both sides are evaluating, there are terms and a decision.

Commercial clarity without relational pressure.

## NSDM-10: The decay loop
**Source:** Ch. 1–2 · **CTA:** Field Guide (TRUST)

Most dead DM threads die in the same loop:

Silence → seller anxiety → more pressure → buyer withdrawal → more silence.

It starts inside you, not the buyer.

Silence feels uncomfortable, so you add urgency, repeat the ask, or send more "value." That gives them more reason to withdraw.

The interruption is a single question before every message:

"Am I sending this because it helps the buyer, or because I'm uncomfortable with the silence?"

Then:
→ Am I following up because they signaled readiness, or because my sequence says so?
→ Am I answering an objection they haven't raised?
→ Am I creating urgency that doesn't exist?

Fail the test and you pause.

One caveat: if they asked you a direct question, answer it. The pause stops pressure. It doesn't excuse hiding.

Try it for one day and count the messages you didn't send. That number tells you how much of your outreach was anxiety.

## NSDM-11: One touch, one trust job
**Source:** Ch. 6 · **CTA:** Field Guide (TRUST)

A buyer asks: "How does the initial review work?"

The reply: a bio, three case studies, a limited-time offer, a budget question and a booking link.

The buyer now has six things to interpret instead of one answer to use.

A focused reply:

"It's a 20-minute conversation about your current inquiry-to-proposal process. No deck or system access needed. The output is a decision on whether a deeper diagnosis is worth it. It's not a full strategy session."

That answers one question: is this safe to explore? Nothing else.

The rule: each message does one primary trust job. Before writing, finish this sentence:

"After reading this, the buyer should understand…"

The rule is about focus, not rationing information. If they ask three connected questions, like fee, scope and start date, answer all three.

# The LinkedIn Lead Engine

## LLE-01: Doubling reach doubles the noise
**Source:** Ch. 1 · **CTA:** Field Guide (ENGINE)

If your posts get engagement but no clients, more reach probably won't fix it.

A qualified lead needs all of these:

Reach × audience fit × authority × problem awareness × trust × a conversion path.

More impressions repair one term. Diagnose the others:

→ The wrong people respond? Fix audience fit and topic.
→ The right people read but can't explain what you do? Fix authority and positioning.
→ Good readers ask questions but never move? Fix the path: your offer, your next step, your replies.

This is why doubling reach so often doubles the noise instead of the pipeline.

The question I ask before writing anything: who, specifically, should stop scrolling, and what should they be able to decide afterward?

## LLE-02: "A LinkedIn study says…"
**Source:** Ch. 2 · **CTA:** Complete Edition · Paid-only material

Every week someone posts "We analyzed 50,000 viral LinkedIn posts. Here's what works."

Before you change how you write, ask five questions:

1. Who collected the data, and when?
2. How were posts selected? What counted as "viral"?
3. Was paid promotion excluded?
4. Did they compare similar authors and topics?
5. Was the outcome reach, engagement, leads or revenue? (Not interchangeable.)

A dataset of only winners can tell you what appears among winners. It can't tell you whether the same features appear in thousands of ignored posts too.

If most viral posts use "I" and "you," that doesn't prove adding "I" creates reach.

Full disclosure: the large viral-post study behind my own book's original concept couldn't pass this check, because I couldn't get its dataset or method. So I didn't cite a single number from it.

Personal, conversational writing is usually good writing. Just don't mistake a correlation in winners for a lever you can pull.

## LLE-03: The evidence capsule
**Source:** Ch. 10 · **CTA:** Complete Edition · Paid-only material

"We increased conversions by 40%."

That sentence makes experienced buyers trust you less, not more.

A results post that builds authority has six parts:

1. Context
2. Baseline
3. Action
4. Time period
5. How you defined the outcome
6. A limitation

Stronger (hypothetical numbers): "In a four-week test, completed inquiry forms rose from 10 of 200 visits to 14 of 200. Small sample, and the traffic mix may have changed, so we can't credit the rewrite for all of it."

That's a 40% relative increase and a 2-percentage-point increase.

Knowing the difference, and saying it out loud, is itself a credibility signal. The buyers who matter can tell.

Authority isn't claiming expertise. It's experience, evidence and insight, shown.

## LLE-04: The awareness mismatch
**Source:** Ch. 11 · **CTA:** Field Guide (ENGINE)

Two posts fail for opposite reasons:

A cold reader sees "Book your strategy call." They don't yet know what problem the call solves.

A ready buyer reads another inspirational post. They still can't find your scope.

Neither is a writing failure. They're matching failures.

Same problem, five rungs of awareness:

Unaware: "Your sales team might not have a lead problem."
Problem aware: "3 reasons your pipeline dries up every quarter."
Solution aware: "Predictable outbound without hiring 10 SDRs."
Provider aware: "What changed after we rebuilt a client's outbound."
Ready to buy: "Who our implementation sprint is for, and what it requires."

The fix isn't forcing everyone through a sequence. It's a portfolio with clear routes: diagnostic posts that point to a resource, case studies that point to service details, and a profile with an obvious entry point.

Look at your last ten posts. Which rung is missing?

## LLE-05: The stranger test
**Source:** Ch. 16 · **CTA:** Field Guide (ENGINE)

"Does my profile look professional?" is the wrong question.

Show your profile to someone who hasn't heard your pitch. Give them 20 seconds. Then ask:

1. Who is this for?
2. What problem does this person solve?
3. What makes that believable?
4. What would you do next?

If they can't answer question 4, your posts are sending people to a dead end.

Most profiles fail at question 2. The headline describes a title, not a problem.

A simple rebuild: headline = audience + problem + method.

Your content does the attracting. Your profile does the confirming. When they don't match, the reader's trust resets to zero right at the moment they were considering you.

Run the test this week. It costs one favor and 20 seconds.

## LLE-06: Define the denominator
**Source:** Ch. 21 · **CTA:** B2B (AUDIT)

"Our LinkedIn conversion rate is 6%."

Six percent of what?

Most content metrics are made up of a real numerator over a flattering, or meaningless, denominator.

Better definitions:

Fit conversation rate = fit conversations ÷ substantive new conversations. Not ÷ impressions. Not ÷ reactions.

Opportunity rate = qualified opportunities ÷ fit conversations.

Proposal win rate = won ÷ decided proposals. Report open proposals separately. An open proposal isn't a loss.

Landing-page conversion = completed requests ÷ measured page sessions. Don't silently swap in LinkedIn link clicks.

And always state the time period and cohort. B2B decisions often land weeks after the post that started them.

If you're a founder or revenue leader and no one can tell you these numbers for your LinkedIn presence, that's the leak.

## LLE-07: Design the lead magnet backward
**Source:** Ch. 14 · **CTA:** Complete Edition · Paid-only material

Most lead magnets attract people who will never buy the service.

"100 cold email templates" attracts people who want templates. If you sell an outbound system, they're not your buyer.

Design backward instead:

1. Write down the paid outcome you deliver.
2. Identify the decision a buyer must make before they'd consider it.
3. Build the resource around that decision.

Predictable outbound (B2B agency) → "Is our problem volume, targeting or conversion?" → a pipeline-leak diagnostic.

Fractional CFO → "Do we understand our runway risk?" → a 12-month cash-scenario template.

Ops consultant → "Is the delay access, ownership, scope or scheduling?" → a handoff checklist.

Then measure the resource, not the downloads. How many requesters were people who could buy?

## LLE-08: Deliver first
**Source:** Ch. 14 · **CTA:** Field Guide (ENGINE)

"Comment GUIDE and I'll DM it to you."

Then the DM arrives: "Before I send it, would you be open to a quick call?"

That's a bait and switch, and the reader knows it.

Three ways to deliver a resource, and when each one fits:

Ungated: when the goal is broad usefulness and low friction.

Email-gated: when ongoing delivery is genuinely part of the value. Say what they're signing up for.

By DM request: only when the conversation adds value and you can fulfill every request promptly.

Then deliver first:

"Here's the worksheet you asked for. Start with the stage where work waits longest. If you tell me which one you picked, I can help you use it."

Never make a discovery call the price of a resource you promised.

## LLE-09: Use AI as an editor, not an author
**Source:** Ch. 7 · **CTA:** Complete Edition (AI Post Coach)

The biggest risk with AI-written LinkedIn posts isn't that they sound robotic.

It's that they sound fluent while implying results you never had.

A fluent paragraph can misrepresent a source, invent a client or round "one booking" up to "consistent pipeline."

The prompt that keeps me honest:

"Preserve these facts. Flag anything that needs evidence. Suggest three clearer openings. Do not add experiences, clients or results."

Give the AI your audience, your thesis, your source notes, the facts you're allowed to use and a sample of your voice. Ask it to find vague claims and unsupported numbers before you ask for a rewrite.

You're still responsible for the final draft.

And anonymizing a client's name doesn't help if the project, dates and figures are distinctive.

## LLE-10: "Thanks! DM me if interested."
**Source:** Ch. 15 · **CTA:** Bundle

"Thanks! DM me if interested."

That's the most common reply to a thoughtful comment on LinkedIn. It ends the conversation.

Classify the comment first:

Appreciation → a genuine thank-you. That's enough.
A specific experience → a question that narrows their situation.
Disagreement → a clarification, a correction or a concession.
A resource request → fulfill it, promptly.
A buying question → a direct commercial answer.

Instead of "DM me if interested":

"Interesting that you mentioned hiring. Is the bigger issue getting enough candidates, or finding ones with the right experience?"

Now you're having a conversation.

Reply to the substance, not the opportunity. Move to DM only when they ask or the details get sensitive, and bring the context with you.

## LLE-11: Big audiences behaved differently
**Source:** My Story · **CTA:** Field Guide (ENGINE)

> **Confirm before posting.** This draws on your internal sourcing notes (see LLE Eden notes, "To verify" item 3).

When we sourced experts for a marketplace, I assumed the biggest LinkedIn accounts would be the best bets.

They weren't.

Accounts with 20,000+ followers often converted worse than people with a few thousand engaged followers.

Many were already monetizing elsewhere. Big audiences looked attractive on paper and behaved differently than we expected.

The people who converted had something else: a specific problem they solved, for a specific kind of person, and an audience that recognized it.

That changed how I think about LinkedIn:

Followers measure distribution. They don't measure relevance.

If you have 2,000 followers and every one of them could plausibly hire you, you're not behind. You may be ahead of the account with 50,000 who can't.

## LLE-12: Track contribution, not sole cause
**Source:** Ch. 21 · **CTA:** B2B (ThriveXLabs)

"Which post brought in this client?"

Usually, none of them alone.

Real B2B buying paths look like this: a post, a referral, several articles, then a reply to an email three weeks later.

Crediting the full deal to the last touch overstates one channel. Crediting it to every touch counts the same revenue four times.

What to do instead:
→ Ask every new prospect how they found you and what made them reach out.
→ Record the first known touch, the content they mention, the immediate trigger, and other channels involved.
→ Report sourced and influenced opportunities separately, under definitions you write down.

Two more things:
Revenue isn't profit. Compare cash collected with delivery costs and the hours content and conversations take.
Pipeline isn't cash, and an arbitrary close probability doesn't make it cash.
