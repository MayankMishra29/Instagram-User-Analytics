## P02: The Six Trust Gates
**Image:** `images/post-02-six-trust-gates.png` · **Keyword:** TRUST

Every buyer in your DMs is silently answering one of six questions:

1. Reality: is this person real?
2. Relevance: is this relevant to me?
3. Fit: do they understand my situation?
4. Safety: is it safe to explore?
5. Value: is it worth it?
6. Identity: is this the right decision for us?

Most "salesy" DMs aren't salesy because of the words.

They're salesy because they answer question 5 for someone still on question 2.

"Hey, we help founders get 10–20 qualified leads a month. Open to a quick call?"

That's a value pitch plus a commitment ask, sent to someone who hasn't decided you're relevant.

The fix isn't softer wording. It's finding the earliest open gate and answering only that one.

If someone asks "Have you worked with a company like ours?", a calendar link is the wrong answer. They asked a fit question. Answer it honestly, including what doesn't transfer.

Three things the gates don't mean:
→ Your content and profile can pass gates 1 and 2 before you ever send a DM.
→ Gates reopen. A surprise term at the end sends people back to Safety.
→ If someone who knows you asks your price, give the price. Don't walk them back through the sequence.

## P03: Interest is not readiness
**Image:** `images/post-03-interest-is-not-readiness.png` · **Keyword:** TRUST

A 46% reply rate taught me the most expensive lesson in outbound:

Replies are interest. Bookings need readiness.

They are different rungs of the same ladder:

Attention: a like, a view, a follow
Curiosity: "Interesting."
Interest: "How would that work?"
Exploration: "What would you recommend for us?"
Intent: "Can you send scope and pricing?"
Readiness: "We have approval. What do you need to start?"

Most outreach fails by treating rung 2 like rung 5. Someone says "interesting" and gets a calendar link.

The quieter failure is the reverse. Someone asks for scope and pricing, and gets another case study. That's not patience. It's hiding. Ambiguity is a risk, and risk stops decisions.

The rule I use:
When they're curious, don't act like a decision is overdue.
When they're ready, stop educating and make a clear, priced offer.

Try it today: place every open thread on the ladder. Anyone at Intent or Readiness gets a direct offer with a price this week.

## P04: Reply rates lie (B2B)
**Image:** `images/post-04-reply-rates-lie.png` · **Keyword:** REPORT

If someone runs outbound for you and reports reply rate as the headline number, ask one question:

"Replies to what, from whom, and what happened next?"

We ran 11,466 cold LinkedIn DMs at a 46% reply rate. On a dashboard, that's a win.

But a reply can be "interesting," "not for us," "who are you?" or "send me something." They all count the same.

Here's what moved the result for us, and none of it was copy:

→ Targeting is the first act of reading the buyer. A company that just raised money and one that just posted a freelance job are at different decision points. Same message, different buyer.
→ Our worst segment wasn't a wording problem. That audience was more technical, more skeptical and already flooded with AI-tool outreach.
→ Small, specific proof traveled further than big claims.

What to measure instead: fit conversations ÷ substantive conversations, qualified opportunities, and won ÷ decided proposals. Each one has a defined denominator and a time period.

## P06: Viral ≠ valuable
**Image:** `images/post-06-viral-vs-valuable.png` · **Keyword:** ENGINE

Two axes decide whether a post is worth writing: reach and relevance.

High reach, low relevance: vanity viral. Huge numbers, nobody who buys.
Low reach, low relevance: noise.
Low reach, high relevance: a quiet pipeline. Underrated.
High reach, high relevance: qualified attention. The goal.

Most LinkedIn advice only optimizes the horizontal axis.

But think about what a lead actually needs:

Reach × audience fit × authority × problem awareness × trust × a conversion path.

More impressions fix exactly one of those terms. If the wrong people respond, fix audience fit. If the right people read but can't explain what you do, fix authority. If good readers never take a next step, fix the path.

That's why doubling reach so often doubles the noise, not the pipeline.

One question before your next post: who, specifically, should stop scrolling, and what should they be able to decide afterward?

If you can't answer it, the post isn't ready. More distribution won't save it.

## P07: The CTA ladder
**Image:** `images/post-07-cta-ladder.png` · **Keyword:** ENGINE

"Agree?" "Thoughts?" "Comment YES."

These ask for effort and give no reason. LinkedIn has said it's reducing this kind of engagement bait.

A better rule: ask for the smallest next step the post has earned.

Climb only as far as the post deserves:

1. Application: "Check one stalled proposal for an owner, a decision and a date."
2. Experience: "What's the biggest bottleneck you're seeing?"
3. Resource: "The checklist is linked below."
4. Conversation: "Tell me the stage where it stalls."
5. Commercial: "If you're solving this this quarter, message me about a review."

A diagnostic post can earn rung 3. A case study with a clear scope can earn rung 5. A hot take earns rung 2 at most.

One more rule: say what the next step costs. If it needs an email, say so. If the call is 20 minutes, say what they'll leave with.

## P08: Pipeline audit (B2B)
**Image:** `images/post-08-pipeline-audit.png` · **Keyword:** AUDIT

Is your LinkedIn producing pipeline, or just posts?

Score it across six stages. Fix the lowest one first:

1. Audience and offer: could a stranger say who you help and with what?
2. Profile and path: does your profile confirm what your posts promise, with an obvious next step?
3. Content mix: are you publishing attraction, authority, relationship and conversion posts, or only one kind?
4. CTAs and lead magnets: does each resource lead to the decision your paid service solves?
5. Conversations: do comments become conversations, and conversations become opportunities?
6. Measurement: can you name your fit-conversation rate with a real denominator?

Most teams I talk to try to fix stage 3 (write more, write better) when the leak is in stage 2 or 5.

More posts into a broken path just make the leak bigger.

## Carousel 1: Hook + Rehook (LLE)
**File:** `carousels/carousel-lle-hook-rehook.pdf` (upload as a LinkedIn document) · **Keyword:** HOOKS → LLE Field Guide · **Doc title:** "The hook earns a glance. The rehook earns the read."

Most people obsess over line one of a LinkedIn post.

The people who get read write lines one to three as a unit.

The hook earns a glance. The rehook (the line before "…see more") earns the read, but only if it adds information.

Three moves that work:
→ Narrow: tell the right reader it's for them.
→ Complicate: add a twist that improves the diagnosis.
→ Preview: set an honest expectation of the payoff.

"You won't believe what happens next" adds nothing. A mechanism, a consequence or a narrower audience adds everything.
